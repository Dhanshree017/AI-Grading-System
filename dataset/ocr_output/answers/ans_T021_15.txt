A-1. Explain the concept of model drift. Discuss different types of drift and why 
continuous monitoring is required. 
Model drift is the decline in an AI model's predictive accuracy over time due to shifts in real
world data patterns, primarily caused by Data Drift (input data changes) and Concept 
Drift (relationship between inputs and outputs changes), requiring continuous 
monitoring to detect these shifts and trigger retraining, ensuring models remain reliable, 
fair, and relevant in dynamic environments.  
Concept of Model Drift 
 Definition: Model drift (or decay) happens when a deployed machine learning model's 
performance degrades because the live data it encounters differs significantly from the 
historical data it was trained on, making its predictions less accurate or useful. 
 Analogy: Imagine a weather model trained on data from a stable climate being used during a 
period of rapid climate change; its predictions become less reliable as the underlying weather 
patterns shift.  
Types of Drift 
1. Data Drift (Feature Drift/Input Drift): 
1. What it is: Changes in the statistical properties (distribution) of the input features. 
2. Example: A bank's loan model sees a sudden influx of applications from a new region, 
altering the distribution of the 'state' feature, or changes in customer demographics. 
2. Concept Drift (Target Drift): 
0. What it is: A change in the relationship between the input features and the target variable 
(what the model predicts). 
1. Example: During an economic recession, what defines 'high-risk' customers changes, 
making the model's old rules for income and credit scores less relevant. 
3. Label Drift (Prediction Drift): 
0. What it is: A shift in the distribution of the model's output predictions, often a symptom of 
data or concept drift. 
1. Example: A fraud detection model suddenly starts flagging significantly more transactions 
as fraudulent, even if input data hasn't changed drastically, indicating the concept of 'fraud' 
itself might be evolving.  
Why Continuous Monitoring is Required 
 Maintains Accuracy & Reliability: Prevents models from making faulty decisions by 
alerting teams when performance drops below acceptable thresholds. 
 Adaptability: Real-world conditions (customer behavior, economic factors, trends) are 
always changing, so models must adapt to remain relevant. 
 Detects New Patterns: Catches emerging trends or sudden shifts (like seasonal changes or 
unexpected events) that the original training data didn't cover. 
 Ensures Fairness & Trust: Helps identify biases that might emerge over time, upholding 
ethical AI practices and building user trust. 
 Triggers Retraining: Provides the necessary signals to retrain or update the model with 
fresh data, keeping it effective.  
In essence, continuous monitoring transforms AI from a static tool into a dynamic, living 
system that evolves with the world it serves.

prices from historical data), while online learning updates incrementally with each new data

Batch Learning (Offline Learning) 
 Process: Gathers all data, trains the model on the entire dataset at once (or large chunks), and 
deploys it. 
 Data: Needs a fixed, complete dataset before training begins. 
 Adaptability: Poor; requires full retraining from scratch for updates, making it slow to adapt 
to changes. 
 Resources: High computational cost (CPU, memory) during periodic training. 
 Best For: Stable data, static problems, non-real-time applications. 
 Example: Training a model to predict house prices using last year's sales data; it's stable and 
updated quarterly.  
Online Learning (Incremental Learning) 
 Process: Learns incrementally from data instances as they arrive, updating the model 
continuously. 
 Data: Handles streaming, constantly changing data. 
 Adaptability: Excellent; evolves with new data without full retraining, good for concept 
drift. 
 Resources: Spreads resource usage, efficient for large data streams. 
 Best For: Dynamic environments, real-time systems, fast-changing data. 
 Example: A spam filter that learns from each new email (spam or not spam) to better catch 
future spam, or a fraud detection system adapting to new fraud patterns instantly.  
Key Takeaway 
Choose Batch for stable, large-scale historical analysis (e.g., monthly sales reports). 
Choose Online for dynamic, real-time systems where data is constantly evolving 
(e.g., recommendation engines, real-time ad bidding).  
A-3. Describe the function of an embedding in machine learning. Why are embeddings 
useful for text, images, or categorical data? 
In machine learning, an embedding transforms discrete, high-dimensional data (like words, 
users, or categories) into dense, low-dimensional numerical vectors, capturing semantic 
meaning where similar items have close vectors in the embedding space, making complex 
data usable by models, efficient, and revealing underlying relationships for tasks like search, 
translation, and recommendations.  
Function of Embeddings 
 Transformation: Converts non-numerical, sparse data (e.g., one-hot encoded words) into 
dense, continuous vectors (lists of numbers). 
 Dimensionality Reduction: Reduces the vast space of possibilities (e.g., millions of words) 
to a manageable, lower-dimensional space, improving model efficiency. 
 Semantic Encoding: Learns to represent the meaning, context, or relationships between 
items, so "king" and "queen" end up near each other.  
Usefulness for Different Data Types 
 Text (NLP): 
o Captures Meaning: Words with similar meanings (e.g., "happy," "joyful") get similar 
vectors, enabling models to understand context and perform tasks like sentiment analysis or 
translation. 
o Enables Search: Finds semantically similar documents or phrases, powering semantic 
search engines. 
 Images: 
o Feature Extraction: Converts complex pixel data into a concise vector that represents visual 
features, allowing models to find similar images (e.g., in a photo search). 
o Content Understanding: Helps models understand visual similarity, useful for image 
retrieval and classification. 
 Categorical Data (e.g., User IDs, Product IDs): 
o Learns Relationships: Represents discrete categories (like users or products) as vectors, 
discovering patterns and similarities (e.g., users who like similar movies). 
o Powers Recommendation Systems: By mapping users and items to the same space, models 
can recommend items that are close to a user's embedding.  
Why They Are Useful 
 Efficiency: Dense, lower-dimensional vectors are computationally cheaper to process than 
sparse, high-dimensional ones. 
 Generalization: Models can generalize better from learned relationships (e.g., knowing 
"king" is to "queen" as "man" is to "woman"). 
 Data Bridging: Provides the essential numerical bridge that allows deep learning models to 
work with complex, real-world data.  
A-4. What is concept learning in ML? Explain how hypothesis space and inductive bias 
influence concept learning. 
Answer: 
Concept learning means model try to learn rule from examples. Concept is basically what 
pattern model is trying to understand. 
Hypothesis space: 
 It is set of all possible models or rules model can choose from. 
 If hypothesis space too small, model can’t learn correct rule. 
 If too big, model gets confused. 
Inductive bias: 
 These are assumptions the learner uses, like preferring simpler rule. 
 Bias helps model choose right hypothesis when many fit data. 
How they influence: 
 If hypothesis space and bias match problem well, model learns correct concept easily. 
 If mismatch, model predicts wrong even if data is correct.

B-1. You are given a small dataset with missing values. Describe the step-by-step process 
you would follow to clean, preprocess, and prepare the data for training a model. 
To clean and preprocess a small dataset with missing values, you'd first understand the 
data, then handle missing data (impute mean/median/mode, use KNN, or delete rows if 
minimal), deal with outliers, encode categorical features, scale numerical features, and 
finally split data into training/testing sets to prepare for modeling. For small datasets, 
imputation over deletion is often preferred, while understanding data context guides choices 
like using median (robust to outliers) or mean.  
Here's a step-by-step process: 
1. Load & Inspect Data: Import libraries (Pandas, NumPy) and load data. 
stats, and identify missing values. 
2. Handle Missing Values: 
Use df.info(), df.describe() , and df.isnull().sum() to understand structure, types, summary 
1. For numerical features: Impute with mean (if distribution is normal) or median (if 
skewed/outliers present). KNN imputation or regression can also predict missing values. 
2. For categorical features: Impute with the mode (most frequent category) or create a new 
"Missing" category. 
3. Deletion: Only remove rows if missing data is very little (e.g., <5%) and non-informative; 
otherwise, imputation preserves data. 
3. Handle Outliers: 
0. Detect: Use boxplots or IQR method. 
1. Treat: Cap (winsorize), transform (log), or remove (carefully). 
4. Encode Categorical Data: Convert text/labels to numbers (e.g., One-Hot Encoding for 
nominal, Label Encoding for ordinal) so models can use them. 
5. Feature Scaling: Normalize (Min-Max scaling to 0-1) or Standardize (Z-score scaling to 
mean=0, std=1) numerical features to bring them to a common scale, preventing bias towards 
larger-range features. 
6. Split Data: Divide into Training (e.g., 80%) and Testing (e.g., 20%) sets 
using train_test_split , ensuring scaling is fitted only on the training data to prevent data 
leakage.
