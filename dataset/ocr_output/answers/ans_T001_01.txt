A-1. Define machine learning and its relationship to other fields like 
statistics and AI. 
Machine Learning (ML) is a subfield of Artificial Intelligence defined formally by Tom 
Mitchell as: a computer program is said to learn from Experience (E) with respect to 
some class of Tasks (T) and performance measure P, if its performance at tasks in T, 
as measured by P, improves with E. The objective is generalization—the model's 
ability to perform accurately on new, unseen data. 
 Relationship to AI: AI is the overarching goal of creating intelligent machines. 
ML is the primary paradigm used to achieve this goal, focusing specifically on 
enabling systems to learn, adapt, and make predictions from data rather than 
relying on explicit programming. 
 Relationship to Statistics: ML is fundamentally rooted in inferential statistics 
and mathematical optimization. While Statistics primarily focuses on inference 
(drawing conclusions and proving hypotheses) and model interpretability, ML 
emphasizes predictive accuracy and algorithmic efficiency. The field of 
Statistical Learning Theory formalizes the relationship between the two. 
A-3. Describe how the decision tree algorithm works, including the role of 
entropy and information gain. 
The Decision Tree (DT) algorithm is a non-parametric, supervised learning method 
used for both classification and regression. It operates by performing recursive 
partitioning of the feature space, resulting in a tree structure where each internal 
node represents a feature test and leaf nodes represent the final decision (class label 
or predicted value). 
 Entropy: In the context of decision trees, Entropy is a measure of impurity or 
randomness within a set of samples. High entropy indicates the data is highly 
mixed (low homogeneity). The goal of the DT algorithm is to find splits that 
minimize the total entropy in the resulting child nodes. 
 Information Gain (IG): This metric quantifies the expected reduction in entropy 
achieved by splitting a dataset based on a specific feature. The DT algorithm 
follows a greedy strategy, always choosing the feature split that yields the 
maximum Information Gain, as this ensures the most effective separation of 
classes at that node. 
A-4. Explain the back-propagation algorithm in the context of neural 
networks. 
Back-propagation (Backward Propagation of Errors) is the core algorithm used to 
train deep neural networks efficiently. It is a method for calculating the gradient of 
the loss function with respect to every single weight in the network, which is 
essential for applying Gradient Descent optimization. 
 Forward Pass: Input data is passed through the network, generating an output 
prediction. The Loss Function is calculated, quantifying the error. 
 Backward Pass: The calculated error signal is propagated backward through 
the network, layer by layer, starting from the output. This relies entirely on 
the Chain Rule of Calculus to determine the partial derivative (gradient) of the 
loss with respect to each weight. 
 Weight Update: Once all gradients are computed, the optimizer (e.g., Stochastic 
Gradient Descent or Adam) updates the weights in the opposite direction of 
the gradient, thereby iteratively minimizing the loss function. 
A-6. Explain the bias-variance tradeoff and its significance. 
The Bias-Variance Tradeoff is a foundational concept that decomposes a model's 
total expected generalization error into three components: Bias squared, Variance, 
and Irreducible Error. It highlights the inherent challenge in simultaneously achieving 
both low model complexity (low variance) and high model fit (low bias). 
 Bias (Systematic Error): Error arising from the model being too simple and 
making incorrect assumptions (e.g., assuming linearity). High Bias leads to 
Underfitting, resulting in poor performance on both training and test data. 
 Variance (Random Error): Error arising from the model being too complex and 
overly sensitive to minor fluctuations or noise in the training data. High 
Variance leads to Overfitting, resulting in excellent training performance but 
poor generalization on test data. 
 Significance: The tradeoff guides model selection and hyperparameter tuning. 
The goal is to find the optimal point of complexity where the sum of Bias 
squared and Variance is minimized, resulting in the lowest possible 
generalization error.

B-1. Define and explain metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-score and 
when to use them. 
These three metrics are crucial for evaluating classification models, particularly on 
imbalanced datasets, as they provide a nuanced view of performance derived from 
the Confusion Matrix. 
 Precision: Measures the accuracy of positive predictions. It answers: "Of all the 
samples the model classified as positive, how many were correct?" You use it 
when the cost of a False Positive (Type I Error) is extremely high (e.g., spam 
filtering, judicial decisions). 
 Recall (Sensitivity): Measures the model's ability to find all positive instances. It 
answers: "Of all the samples that were actually positive, how many did the 
model correctly retrieve?" You use it when the cost of a False Negative (Type II 
Error) is extremely high (e.g., disease detection, fraud detection). 
 F1-Score: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall. It provides a single, 
balanced metric that severely penalizes models with uneven performance in 
Precision and Recall. It is the ideal metric when seeking balanced performance 
on imbalanced data. 
B-2. What is a confusion matrix and how is it used to evaluate a 
classification model? 
A confusion matrix is a tabular summary that provides a complete breakdown of a 
classification model's performance on a set of test data. It clearly shows the four 
possible prediction outcomes: True Positives (TP), True Negatives (TN), False Positives 
(FP), and False Negatives (FN). 
 Usage in Evaluation: It serves as the foundational diagnostic tool. By examining 
the counts of False Positives and False Negatives, a developer can immediately 
identify the specific failure mode of the model (e.g., whether it struggles with 
over-classification or under-classification). All other critical classification 
metrics (Precision, Recall, F1) are calculated directly from the values within 
this matrix. 
B-4. How do you select the right machine learning algorithm for a given 
problem? 
Algorithm selection is an iterative, multi-criteria process that balances predictive 
performance with practical constraints. 
 Define the Task: First, determine the problem type: Classification (discrete 
output) or Regression (continuous output). This eliminates the majority of 
irrelevant algorithms. 
 Analyze Data Characteristics: For simple, linear data, choose interpretable 
models like Linear Regression. For large, complex, non-linear data, prioritize 
high-capacity models like Gradient Boosting or Deep Neural Networks. 
 Evaluate Constraints: Consider Interpretability (use transparent models like 
simple Decision Trees if explainability is required) and Latency (use models with 
fast inference times for real-time applications). 
4. Benchmarking: Test multiple suitable candidates using rigorous k-fold cross
validation and select the model that achieves the best score on the target 
business metric while satisfying all operational constraints. 
B-5. Explain the steps in the machine learning model development lifecycle. 
The Machine Learning Model Development Lifecycle (MLOps) is a structured process 
required to move a project from research and experimentation into a stable, 
maintainable production environment. 
 Data Preparation: This involves data cleaning (handling missing data, outliers), 
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA), and Feature Engineering—the process of 
transforming raw variables into highly predictive input features. 
 Model Training and Selection: The data is partitioned into training, validation, 
and test sets. Models are trained on the training set, and hyperparameters are 
tuned on the validation set to select the optimal configuration. 
 Evaluation: The finalized model is evaluated once on the held-out test set to 
obtain an unbiased measure of its generalization performance against the 
business objective. 
 Deployment and Monitoring: The validated model is deployed to production. 
Continuous monitoring is essential to track performance in real-time and 
detect Model Drift (accuracy degradation due to changing input data 
characteristics), triggering necessary retraining.

C-1. What is the role of feature scaling in machine learning. 
Feature Scaling is a critical data preprocessing step used to standardize or normalize 
the range of independent numerical variables. Its primary role is to ensure numerical 
stability and prevent features with larger magnitudes from unfairly dominating the 
model's objective function. 
 Distance-Based Algorithms: It is mandatory for algorithms based on distance 
metrics, such as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) and K-Means Clustering, to ensure 
that distance calculations are not skewed by the arbitrary units of the features. 
 Optimization: It significantly improves the convergence speed of algorithms 
that use Gradient Descent (e.g., Logistic Regression, Neural Networks) by 
ensuring that the loss function surface is more spherical, leading to faster 
minimization.

C-3. Define and explain overfitting and underfitting and how to avoid them. 
Overfitting and Underfitting are the two main manifestations of poor generalization 
arising from a mismatch between model complexity and data complexity. 
 Overfitting: 
o Definition: The model is too complex and fits the training data too 
exactly, learning noise rather than the signal (High Variance). 
o Symptom: Excellent training score, but poor test score. 
o Avoidance: Use Regularization (L1/L2), implement Early Stopping, or 
obtain more training data. 
 Underfitting: 
o Definition: The model is too simple and fails to capture the fundamental 
relationships in the data (High Bias). 
o Symptom: Poor score on both training and test sets. 
o Avoidance: Increase model complexity (e.g., use a non-linear model) or 
perform better Feature Engineering. 
C-4. How to deal with missing data, scale features, or encode categorical 
variables. 
These are the essential transformations required to prepare raw data for machine 
learning algorithms. 
 Dealing with Missing Data (Imputation): For numerical data, use the median 
(more robust to outliers) or mean. For categorical data, use the mode (most 
frequent) or treat "Missing" as its own separate category. 
 Scaling Features: Apply either Standardization ($\text{mean}=0, 
\text{variance}=1$) for gradient-based methods, or Normalization (scaling to 
$\text{[0, 1]}$) for specific algorithms or bounded activation functions. 
 Encoding Categorical Variables: For nominal variables (no order), use One-Hot 
Encoding (creating $\text{N}$ binary columns for $\text{N}$ categories). For 
ordinal variables (has a rank/order), use Label Encoding (assigning rank 
integers, e.g., 1, 2, 3).
