A-1. Explain the role of assumptions in machine learning models and analyze how incorrect assumptions 
can affect model behavior and decision-making.  
Assumptions in machine learning, often referred to as Inductive Bias, are the prior premises an algorithm 
uses to predict outputs for inputs it has never encountered. These assumptions are essential because, without 
them, a learner would have no basis for generalization.  
• Role of Assumptions: Algorithms assume a specific structure in the data to make learning 
computationally feasible. For example, Linear Regression assumes a linear relationship between 
variables ($y = mx + c$), while Naive Bayes assumes that all input features are independent of each 
other. These "short-cuts" allow the model to search through a limited Hypothesis Space rather than 
an infinite one.  
• Impact of Incorrect Assumptions: When the underlying reality of the data contradicts the model's 
assumptions, it leads to Model Misspecification.  
o High Bias (Underfitting): If you use a linear model for data that is inherently non-linear 
(like circular or wave patterns), the model will consistently miss the trend.  
o Poor Decision-Making: In real-world applications, such as credit scoring, an incorrect 
assumption about the relationship between "income" and "risk" could lead to a system that 
systematically denies loans to qualified individuals, resulting in lost business and unfair bias.  
• Analysis: An algorithm is only as good as its bias. If the bias is too strong (too many assumptions), 
the model is rigid; if it is too weak (no assumptions), the model cannot learn at all.  
A-3. 
Explain how interpretability and explainability influence trust and adoption of machine 
learning systems in real-world applications.  
As machine learning moves into high-stakes areas like healthcare and criminal justice, the "Black Box" 
nature of complex models (like Deep Learning) becomes a barrier to adoption.  
• Interpretability: This refers to the degree to which a human can understand the internal mechanics 
of a model (e.g., looking at a Decision Tree and seeing exactly which "if-then" rule was followed).  
• Explainability (XAI): This refers to techniques (like SHAP or LIME) that provide a reason for a 
specific output after it has been generated. For instance, "The loan was denied because the debt
toincome ratio was too high."  
• Trust and Adoption: * Safety: In medicine, a doctor will not trust a diagnosis unless they can see 
which features (like a specific shadow on an X-ray) the AI used.  
o Legal Compliance: Regulations like GDPR give users the "Right to Explanation." Without 
explainability, a company cannot legally deploy certain AI models.  
o Bias Detection: Explainability allows developers to see if a model is making decisions based 
on "protected" categories (like race or gender), allowing them to fix unfair systems before 
they cause harm.  
A-4. 
Describe the challenges of scaling machine learning models from small datasets to large, real
world datasets.  
Scaling an ML model from a small pilot project to a large-scale real-world application is not a simple linear 
process; it introduces several "bottlenecks."  
• Computational Complexity: Some algorithms that work well on 1,000 rows (like certain types of 
clustering or SVMs) have $O(n^2)$ or $O(n^3)$ complexity. This means as the data grows 10x, the 
time needed grows 100x or 1,000x, making them impossible to run on millions of rows.  
• Data Quality Dilution: Small datasets are often hand-picked and clean. Large datasets are usually 
"noisy," containing many missing values, duplicates, and incorrect entries that require massive 
infrastructure for cleaning.  
• Infrastructure & Latency: Real-world scaling requires moving from a single laptop to distributed 
clusters (like Spark or AWS). Managing data "sharding" and ensuring the model can give a 
prediction in milliseconds (latency) is a major engineering challenge.  
• Storage Costs: Storing and versioning massive amounts of data and model weights requires 
significant financial investment and complex database management.
