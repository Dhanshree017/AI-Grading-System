A-2. Describe the working of a Decision Tree algorithm. Explain how decisions are made and discuss its 
advantages and limitations. 
A Decision Tree is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm used for 
both classification and regression tasks. It uses a flowchart-like structure to model decisions and their 
potential consequences, mirroring human decision-making processes by breaking down complex choices 
into a series of simpler, sequential "if-then" questions.  
How the Algorithm Works 
The algorithm builds a tree through a process called recursive partitioning, where the dataset is repeatedly 
split into smaller subsets based on feature values.  
1. Root Node Selection: The process begins with the entire dataset at the "root" (topmost node). The algorithm 
evaluates all available features to find the one that best splits the data into the most distinct or "pure" groups. 
2. Splitting: The chosen feature divides the data into branches based on its possible values (e.g., "Income > 
$50,000" vs. "Income ≤ $50,000"). 
3. Recursive Branching: This splitting process is repeated for each resulting child node. At each step, the 
algorithm seeks the most significant attribute to further refine the subsets. 
4. Stopping Criteria: The splitting continues until a stopping condition is met, such as: 
1. All samples in a node belong to the same class (a "pure" node). 
2. A maximum tree depth is reached. 
3. The number of samples in a node falls below a certain threshold. 
5. Leaf Nodes: These are the final terminal nodes that do not split further; they represent the final decision or 
prediction.  
How Decisions (Splits) are Made 
Decisions are guided by Attribute Selection Measures (ASM), which identify the most informative 
features to maximize the homogeneity of the resulting subsets.  
 Entropy and Information Gain: Used primarily by the ID3 and C4.5 algorithms. Entropy measures the 
disorder or uncertainty in a dataset; Information Gain measures the reduction in entropy after a split. 
Features with the highest Information Gain are preferred. 
 Gini Impurity: Used by the CART algorithm. It measures the probability of a randomly chosen element 
being incorrectly classified. Splits that result in the lowest Gini Impurity (greatest purity) are chosen. 
 Variance Reduction: Used for regression tasks to minimize the variance of target values within each 
subset.  
Advantages 
 Interpretability: Decisions are easy to visualize and explain, even to non-experts, as they follow a clear 
logical path. 
 Minimal Data Preparation: Unlike many other algorithms, decision trees do not require data 
normalization, scaling, or extensive cleaning. 
 Versatility: They handle both numerical and categorical data naturally and can be used for both 
classification and regression. 
 Inbuilt Feature Selection: The algorithm inherently identifies the most important features during the 
construction process.  
Limitations 
 Overfitting: Deep trees can become overly complex, memorizing noise in the training data rather than 
general patterns, which leads to poor performance on new data. 
 Instability: Small changes in the training data can result in a completely different tree structure. 
 Biased Splits: Algorithms like ID3 are biased toward features with many levels (many possible values). 
 Greedy Approach: Because they make locally optimal decisions at each node, they do not guarantee a 
globally optimal tree.

A-3. Explain the bias–variance tradeoff. Discuss its impact on underfitting and overfitting with practical 
illustrations. 
The Bias-Variance Tradeoff is the core challenge in machine learning: you can't minimize both bias (errors 
from oversimplified models, causing underfitting) and variance (errors from overly complex models fitting 
noise, causing overfitting) simultaneously; instead, you find an optimal balance where a model is complex 
enough to learn patterns but simple enough to generalize well, preventing it from memorizing training data 
noise. High bias leads to poor training/testing performance (underfitting), while high variance leads to great 
training performance but poor testing performance (overfitting).  
Bias vs. Variance 
 Bias: The error from wrong assumptions in the learning algorithm, causing it to miss relevant relations (e.g., 
using a straight line for curved data). 
o High Bias (Underfitting): Model is too simple, makes strong assumptions, performs poorly on training and 
test data. 
 Variance: The error from sensitivity to small fluctuations in the training set, leading to fitting noise (e.g., a 
wiggly line through every point). 
o High Variance (Overfitting): Model is too complex, fits training data perfectly but fails on new data (poor 
generalization).  
Practical Illustrations 
Imagine predicting house prices based on size: 
1. Underfitting (High Bias): Using a simple linear regression (straight line) to predict prices for many data 
points that actually follow a curve. The line misses the pattern, performing poorly even on data it trained on 
(e.g., predicts low prices for large houses). 
2. Overfitting (High Variance): Using a very deep decision tree that creates a unique rule for every single 
house in the training set. It perfectly predicts training prices but fails on new houses because it learned their 
specific quirks, not general trends (e.g., a house with 3 windows vs. 4 windows). 
3. Optimal Fit: A moderately complex model (perhaps a slightly curved line or a shallower tree) that captures 
the general upward trend in price with size but ignores minor noise. It performs well on both training and 
new data.  
The Tradeoff in Action 
 Simple Model (Low Complexity): High Bias, Low Variance (Underfitting). 
 Complex Model (High Complexity): Low Bias, High Variance (Overfitting).  
The goal is to find the "sweet spot" where the total error (Bias² + Variance + Irreducible Error) is 
minimized, achieved by adjusting model complexity (e.g., regularization, feature selection, cross
validation).

A-4. Discuss the importance of model evaluation in machine learning. Explain why accuracy alone is not 
sufficient and describe other evaluation measures. 
We evaluate to make sure the model is not a complete disaster before we deploy it. 
 Importance: It tells you if the model actually works outside of the training room and helps you 
choose which model is the least garbage. 
Why Accuracy Alone is Not Sufficient: 
 Accuracy is just the "got it right" score. 
 It's useless when the data is imbalanced. If only one person in a crowd has a hidden treasure, and I 
just guess "no treasure" for everyone, I'll be $99\%$ accurate but I failed the one important job. 
Other Evaluation Measures: 
We use a Confusion Matrix to look at the details. 
1. Recall: This is about finding all the important stuff. How many of the actual treasures did I find? 
You need high recall for things like catching criminals or finding diseases. 
2. Precision: This is about being correct when I claim something. If I say "treasure here!", how often 
am I right? You need high precision when you don't want to make false alarms, like saying someone 
is guilty when they are not. 
3. F1-Score: This is just a mix of the two above, to keep things balanced.

A-6. Describe how machine learning models are deployed and maintained in real-world systems. Explain 
issues such as monitoring, retraining, and performance degradation. 
In real-world systems, machine learning (ML) model deployment and maintenance are managed through a 
discipline known as MLOps (Machine Learning Operations). This process bridges the gap between 
experimental data science and production software engineering, ensuring models remain accurate, scalable, 
and reliable after they leave the development lab.  
1. Deployment Strategies 
Modern deployment involves packaging a trained model into a production environment where it can receive 
live data and return predictions.  
 Containerization: Models and their dependencies (libraries, code) are bundled into containers 
(e.g., Docker) to ensure they run identically across different environments. 
 Serving Patterns: 
o Real-time Inference: Models are hosted behind APIs (e.g., FastAPI, Flask) to provide instant predictions 
for applications like fraud detection or recommendation engines. 
o Batch Prediction: Models process large volumes of data at once on a schedule (e.g., daily sales forecasts). 
o Edge Deployment: Models run directly on local hardware like IoT devices or smartphones for low latency 
and offline use. 
 Release Strategies: Techniques like Canary Deployments (sending a small portion of traffic to the new 
model) or Blue-Green Deployments (instantly switching traffic between old and new versions) minimize 
the risk of system failures.  
2. Monitoring and Performance Degradation 
Unlike traditional software, ML models start to degrade almost immediately after deployment because they 
are sensitive to real-world changes.  
 Types of Degradation (Drift): 
o Data Drift: The statistical properties of input data change over time (e.g., customers suddenly buy different 
products due to a new trend). 
o Concept Drift: The fundamental relationship between inputs and outputs changes (e.g., what was previously 
considered "normal" behavior becomes "fraudulent" due to a new scamming technique). 
 Monitoring Key Metrics: 
o Model Performance: Tracking accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in real-time. 
o System Health: Monitoring latency (speed of prediction), throughput, and resource usage like 
CPU/Memory. 
o Data Quality: Identifying missing values, outliers, or format changes in live data streams.  
3. Retraining and Continuous Maintenance 
To combat degradation, organizations must establish a plan for model updates.  
 Retraining Strategies: 
o Schedule-Based: Retraining happens at fixed intervals, such as weekly or monthly. 
o Trigger-Based: Retraining is automatically initiated when performance drops below a certain threshold or 
significant drift is detected. 
o Manual (Retrain on Demand): Teams manually intervene and retrain when they spot specific issues or 
new business requirements. 
 Automated Pipelines: MLOps systems use CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment) to 
automate the entire lifecycle—from validating new data and retraining the model to testing it in a staging 
environment and redeploying it.
