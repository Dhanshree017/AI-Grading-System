A-2. Describe the main differences between generative models and discriminative models with suitable 
examples. 
Generative models learn the joint probability distribution P(x,y)P(x, y)P(x,y) and can generate new samples 
similar to the original dataset. Examples include Naive Bayes, Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), and 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). 
Discriminative models focus on learning the decision boundary by estimating P(y∣x)P(y | x)P(y∣x). They 
excel in classification and regression tasks. Examples include Logistic Regression, SVMs, and Random 
Forests. 
In summary, generative models model data creation and can synthesize samples, while discriminative 
models separate classes and provide more direct predictive accuracy. 
A-3. What is anomaly detection? Discuss its importance and common applications in real-world systems. 
Anomaly detection identifies observations that diverge significantly from normal patterns. These anomalies 
often indicate critical events such as fraud, failures, or cyberattacks. It is important because anomalies 
typically represent rare but high-impact situations that standard models may overlook. 
Common applications include: 
• Fraud detection in banking transactions 
• Intrusion detection in cybersecurity networks 
• Fault detection in manufacturing machinery 
• Disease abnormality detection in medical diagnostics 
• Monitoring sensor data in IoT systems 
Anomaly detection enhances safety, security, and operational reliability. 
A-4. Explain the concept of model interpretability. Why do certain industries require highly 
interpretable ML models? 
Model interpretability refers to the clarity with which one can understand how a model arrives at its 
predictions. It ensures transparency in decision-making and helps verify correctness, fairness, and regulatory 
compliance. 
Industries such as healthcare, finance, insurance, and legal sectors require interpretable models because 
decisions directly affect human lives, financial outcomes, and legal accountability. Interpretability allows 
auditors, regulators, and domain experts to validate reasoning, detect bias, and ensure trustworthiness. In 
such fields, explainability is as important as accuracy. 
A-5. Describe the importance of proper dataset splitting. Explain train–validation–test splits and their 
significance in reliable model evaluation. 
Proper dataset splitting ensures unbiased evaluation and prevents data leakage. 
• Training set — used to learn model parameters. 
• Validation set — used to tune hyperparameters, optimize architecture, and detect overfitting. 
• Test set — used only at the end to evaluate final model performance. 
This separation allows reliable estimation of generalization ability, ensures hyperparameter choices do not 
bias results, and maintains scientific rigor in model evaluation. A well-structured split is essential for 
reproducibility and trustworthy performance metrics.

B-1. Explain the role of Data Preprocessing in building ML models. Discuss key steps such as data 
cleaning, encoding, transformation, and handling outliers. 
Data preprocessing prepares raw data into an optimal form for machine learning. 
• Data cleaning: handles missing values, removes duplicates, fixes incorrect entries, and resolves 
inconsistencies. 
• Encoding: converts categorical variables into numerical representations using one-hot encoding, label 
encoding, or embeddings. 
• Transformation: scaling, normalization, and log-transforms improve convergence and stabilize numerical 
relationships. 
• Handling outliers: removing, capping, or transforming extreme values prevents distortion of model 
learning. 
Proper preprocessing improves accuracy, prevents bias, enhances training efficiency, and produces robust 
models. 
B-3. Explain how ML models are used in Natural Language Processing (NLP). Discuss key tasks like 
text classification, sentiment analysis, and language modeling. 
Machine learning powers most modern NLP applications. 
• Text classification assigns categories to documents, such as spam detection or topic classification. 
• Sentiment analysis identifies emotional tone in text, widely used for product review mining and social 
media analysis. 
• Language modeling predicts the next word or generates coherent text, forming the foundation for chatbots 
and translation systems. 
ML models transform text into embeddings, capture semantic meaning, and learn patterns that enable 
machines to understand and produce human language. 
B-4. Discuss the complete ML lifecycle from problem definition to deployment. Explain the purpose of 
each stage with appropriate examples. 
The ML lifecycle includes: 
1. Problem definition: identifying the objective—e.g., predicting loan default. 
2. Data collection: gathering relevant structured or unstructured data. 
3. Preprocessing: cleaning, encoding, scaling, and organizing features. 
4. Model development: training ML models and tuning hyperparameters. 
5. Evaluation: assessing performance using metrics and validation strategies. 
6. Deployment: integrating the model into applications or APIs. 
7. Monitoring and retraining: tracking performance, detecting drift, updating models. 
Each stage ensures reliability, scalability, and continuous improvement of the solution. 
B-5. What is Unsupervised Learning? Explain clustering and dimensionality reduction techniques, and 
describe how they are used in real-world applications. 
Unsupervised learning discovers hidden structure in unlabeled data. 
• Clustering: groups similar data points (e.g., customer segmentation, document grouping). 
• Dimensionality reduction: reduces feature space while preserving information (e.g., PCA for noise 
reduction, t-SNE for visualization). 
Applications include recommendation systems, anomaly detection, compression, biological pattern analysis, 
and market segmentation. 
B-6. Explain the concept of Reinforcement Learning. Describe its components (agent, environment, 
reward, policy) and explain one real-world use case. 
Reinforcement learning (RL) trains an agent to make sequential decisions by interacting with an 
environment. 
• Agent: decision-maker 
• Environment: setting where actions occur 
• Reward: signal guiding desired actions 
• Policy: strategy mapping states to actions 
A real-world example is robotic control, where a robot learns to navigate obstacles by receiving rewards for 
correct movement and penalties for collisions.

C-1. Discuss the challenges of deploying ML models in real-world environments. Explain issues like data 
drift, model monitoring, scalability, hardware constraints, security risks, and continuous retraining . 
Real-world deployment introduces challenges not present during offline experimentation. 
• Data drift: changes in data distributions reduce accuracy over time. 
• Model monitoring: continuous tracking of latency, prediction quality, and feature distributions ensures 
early detection of issues. 
• Scalability: production systems must handle large traffic, requiring distributed processing and optimized 
infrastructure. 
• Hardware constraints: edge devices, mobile apps, and IoT systems require lightweight, optimized 
models. 
• Security risks: adversarial attacks, data poisoning, model theft, and privacy leaks threaten system 
reliability. 
• Continuous retraining: as environments change, updated models must be retrained, validated, and 
redeployed without disrupting service. 
Deployment success depends on balancing accuracy, efficiency, security, and robustness. 
C-2. Explain in detail how machine learning is used in healthcare. Describe applications (diagnosis, 
prediction, medical imaging, drug discovery), challenges (data quality, privacy, bias), and the steps 
involved in developing an ML-based healthcare system. 
ML transforms healthcare by improving decision-making and supporting clinicians. 
• Diagnosis: ML identifies early signs of diseases from EHR data or symptoms. 
• Prediction: models predict risk of conditions such as stroke, diabetes, or readmissions. 
• Medical imaging: CNNs interpret X-rays, MRIs, and CT scans with high accuracy. 
• Drug discovery: ML accelerates molecule screening and therapy development. 
Challenges include inconsistent medical records, strict privacy regulations, and algorithmic bias affecting 
fairness. 
Steps for building an ML-based healthcare system: 
1. Data collection from hospitals, sensors, imaging systems 
2. Preprocessing and integration of structured/unstructured data 
3. Feature engineering and selection 
4. Model training and validation 
5. Clinical testing and expert review 
6. Deployment in hospital workflows 
7. Continuous monitoring for safety, accuracy, and ethical compliance
