A-1. Define supervised, unsupervised, reinforcement? 
Machine Learning is defined by three fundamental paradigms, distinguished by the 
nature of the data available and the feedback mechanism used for iterative learning. 
1. Supervised Learning: 
o Definition: The algorithm learns a mapping function f:X→Y from a 
labeleddataset, where every input X has a corresponding ground-truth 
output Y. The training objective is lossminimization (e.g., Cross-Entropy or 
MSE) between the predicted and true output. 
o Goal: Prediction of known outcomes (Classification for discrete labels, 
Regression for continuous values). 
2. Unsupervised Learning: 
o Definition: The algorithm operates on unlabeleddata and must 
autonomously discoverintrinsicstructures, patterns, or associations within 
the dataset. There is no external error signal; success is judged by internal 
metrics like distance or density. 
o Goal: Data exploration, structure discovery, and data compression (e.g., 
Clustering, Dimensionality Reduction). 
3. Reinforcement Learning (RL): 
o Definition: An intelligent agent learns optimal behavior through 
trial−and−error interaction with a dynamic environment. The agent's goal 
is 
to 
learn 
an 
optimal policy that dictates actions to 
maximizetheexpectedcumulativediscountedreward signal over time. 
o Goal: Sequential decision-making under uncertainty, balancing the 
exploration−exploitationtradeoff. 
A-3. What is the difference between parametric and non-parametric models? 
The distinction rests on the assumptions made about the data's underlying functional 
form. 
Parametric Models 
 Assumption: They assume a fixed,finitefunctionalform for the relationship 
between X and Y (e.g., a linear relationship). This translates to a 
fixednumberofparameters (coefficients) regardless of the dataset size. 
 Data Dependence: Once the parameters are learnedandfixed, the original 
training data can be discarded, making them memory efficient. 
 Characteristics: Tend to have highbias (if assumptions are wrong) and 
lowvariance (due to their fixed structure). They are highlyinterpretable and 
computationally fast at inference. 
 Example: LinearRegression (which learns two parameters: slope and intercept). 
Non-Parametric Models 
 Assumption: They makenoassumptions about the functional form, allowing their 
structure to be highlyflexible and adapt to complex, non-linear patterns. 
 Data Dependence: The number of effective parameters is notfixed and often 
growswiththetrainingdatasize. They typically requiretheentiretrainingdataset to 
be stored for prediction. 
 Characteristics: Tend to have lowbias (due to flexibility) and highvariance (prone 
to overfitting noise). They are generally lessinterpretable and can be slower at 
prediction. 
 Example: K−NearestNeighbors(KNN), DecisionTrees.

A-5. What is underfitting, and how can you prevent it? 
Underfitting occurs when a model is toosimple to capture the fundamental, governing 
patterns in the data. The model failstolearn the data's complexity and exhibits 
poorperformance on boththetrainingsetandthetestset. It is associated with highbias and 
lowvariance. 
Underfitting is typically caused by using a model with insufficient capacity or applying 
excessive constraints. To prevent it: 
 Increase Model Complexity: Switch to a higher−capacityalgorithm (e.g., moving 
from Linear Regression to a Support Vector Machine or a multi-layered Neural 
Network). 
 Add Relevant Features: Perform better featureengineering by incorporating non
linear interactions or domain-specific variables that help the model separate the 
data. 
 Decrease Regularization: If a regularization penalty (L1/L2) is being used, 
reducethestrength (λ) of the penalty term to allow the model's weights to 
increase and capture more complexity. 
 Remove Noise: If the data is heavily contaminated, cleaning or removingoutliers 
can help a simple model fit the core, reliable patterns more effectively.

B-1. Explain the bias-variance tradeoff. 
The bias-variance tradeoff is a core theoretical framework that decomposes the 
totalexpectedgeneralizationerror of a predictive model into three components:  
Total Error=Bias2+Variance+Irreducible Error 
The tradeoff requires balancing Bias (systematic error due to faulty assumptions) and 
Variance (random error due to sensitivity to training data fluctuations). 
1. Bias (Systematic Error): 
o High Bias: Occurs when the model is toosimple (low capacity) for the 
complexity of the data, leading to underfitting. The model cannot fit the 
training data well. 
o Mitigation: Increasing model complexity. 
2. Variance (Random Error): 
o High Variance: Occurs when the model is toocomplex (high capacity), 
making it overly sensitive to noise, leading to overfitting. The model 
performs well on training data but poorly on test data. 
o Mitigation: Decreasing model complexity (e.g., regularization). 
The tradeoff exists because increasing model complexity to reducebias (better fit to 
data) will inherently increase the model's variance (higher sensitivity to noise). The goal 
of model selection and hyperparameter tuning is to find the SweetSpot—the optimal 
complexity level where the sum of Bias2 and Variance is at its minimum, guaranteeing 
the best possible generalization error. 
B-2. How does the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm work? 
The K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm is a non−parametric and instance−based 
learning algorithm that operates under the "lazy learning" principle. 
Mechanism 
1. Lazy Training: The algorithm performsnoexplicittraining or model building phase. 
It simply storestheentiretrainingdataset and defers all computation until a 
prediction is requested. 
2. Distance Metric: When a new data point Xnew arrives, the algorithm calculates 
the distance between Xnew and everysinglepoint in the stored training dataset. 
The EuclideanDistance is the most common metric. 
3. K Selection: It identifies the K instances from the training set that have the 
smallest distances to Xnew. The hyperparameter K determines the size of the 
local neighborhood considered. 
4. Prediction: 
o Classification: The output class of Xnew is determined by a majorityvote 
among the K nearest neighbors. 
o Regression: The predicted output value is the average or median of the 
target values of the K nearest neighbors. 
Crucial Prerequisites 
KNN's reliance on distance makes featurescaling mandatory (e.g., using standardization 
or normalization). Without it, features with larger numerical ranges would 
disproportionately dominate the distance calculation, rendering the results 
meaningless.

B-4. How would you choose an algorithm for a specific dataset? 
Algorithm selection is an iterative, multi-criteria decision process guided by the 
problem's nature and practical constraints. 
1. Problem Definition and Task Type: 
o First, determine the output type: Classification (discrete labels) or 
Regression (continuous values). This immediately filters the candidate pool. 
o If no target variable exists, the task is Unsupervised (Clustering or PCA). 
2. Data Analysis and Feature Characteristics: 
o Data Volume: For smalldatasets, favor simpler, high-bias models (e.g., 
Linear Models) to prevent overfitting. For massivedatasets, high-capacity 
and scalable models (e.g., Deep Learning, Gradient Boosting) are 
appropriate. 
o Linearity: If relationships are clearly linear, start with LinearModels (fast, 
interpretable). If the data is non−linear, use Tree−basedensembles (like 
Random Forest) or KernelMethods (e.g., RBF-SVM). 
o Outliers/Noise: If the data is noisy, models robust to outliers, like 
DecisionTrees, should be prioritized over distance-based models (KNN) or 
mean-based models (K-Means). 
3. Evaluate Constraints and Deployment: 
o Interpretability: If modelexplainability is legally or ethically required (e.g., 
loan decisions), sacrifice some accuracy for transparentmodels (e.g., Logistic 
Regression) or implement post−hocexplanation tools (SHAP). 
o Latency: For real−timedeployment, select models with lowinferencetime 
(fast prediction speed), which often favors simpler models.

B-5. Explain bagging and boosting. 
Bagging and Boosting are the two most prominent techniques in EnsembleLearning, 
where the goal is to combine the predictions of multiple weaklearners (base models) to 
achieve a superiorstronglearner. They differ fundamentally in how the base models are 
trained. 
1. Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) 
 Mechanism: Base models (usually Decision Trees) are trained independently and 
inparallel. Each base model is trained on a different bootstrapsample—a random 
subset of the original training data drawn withreplacement. 
 Goal: The final prediction is determined by averaging the outputs (regression) or 
taking a majorityvote (classification). The primary objective of bagging is to 
reducevariance and preventoverfitting by decorrelating the errors of the 
individual base models. 
 Example: RandomForest (which adds an extra layer of randomness by using a 
random subset of features for splitting). 
2. Boosting 
 Mechanism: Base models are trained sequentially (one after the other) and 
dependently. Each subsequent model focusesspecificallyoncorrectingtheerrors 
made by the combined ensemble of the preceding models. This is achieved by 
reweighting the data, giving higher importance to the misclassified samples. 
 Goal: The primary objective is to reducebias and transform a collection of simple, 
weak learners into a single, highly accurate predictor through iterative error 
correction. 
 Example: GradientBoostingMachines(GBM) and its highly optimized derivative, 
XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting).
