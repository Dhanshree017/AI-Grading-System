A-2. What is data preprocessing and why is it important? 
Data preprocessing is the sequence of cleaning, transforming and encoding raw inputs into 
a consistent, model-ready form (imputation, normalization, encoding, outlier treatment, 
feature transforms). It is vital because models are sensitive to scale, missingness and noise 
— careful preprocessing reduces bias, speeds convergence, prevents numerical issues and 
often yields larger performance gains than marginal model tuning. 
A-3. What are hyperparameters? Give two examples. 
Hyperparameters are configuration choices set before training that control model capacity 
or the optimization process (not learned from data). Examples: learning rate (step size of 
gradient updates) and regularization strength (L1/L2 coefficient controlling weight 
penalty). Other examples include batch size, number of layers, and number of trees.

A-5. What is classification and regression? Explain with examples. 
Classification maps inputs to discrete categories (e.g., detect pneumonia vs normal in chest 
X-rays). Regression predicts continuous targets (e.g., forecast monthly electricity 
consumption in kWh). Choice of loss and evaluation differs (cross-entropy/AUC for 
classification; MSE/MAE/R² for regression). 
A-6. Advantages and disadvantages of Decision Tree 
Advantages: interpretable rules, mixed-type features support, no heavy scaling required, fast 
inference. 
Disadvantages: prone to overfitting unless pruned, high variance (unstable splits), greedy 
split criterion may be locally suboptimal, bias toward features with many levels. 
A-7. How to make a model underfit and overfit (what causes each) 
Underfit causes: too-simple hypothesis class, excessive regularization, lack of informative 
features, insufficient training time. 
Overfit causes: overly expressive model relative to data, noisy labels, no/weak 
regularization, too many training epochs or small training set causing memorization.

B-1. Challenges in machine learning — imbalanced data, missing values, curse of 
dimensionality, concept drift — and solutions 
Imbalanced Data 
 Problem: rare classes (fraud, disease) get overwhelmed by majority, accuracy 
misleading. 
 Solutions: resampling (SMOTE/ADASYN oversampling, informed undersampling), 
cost-sensitive loss (class weights, focal loss), anomaly detection framing for extreme 
rarity, evaluation by PR-AUC and F1 rather than accuracy. 
Missing Values 
 Problem: reduces usable data and can bias estimates if missingness is informative 
(MAR/MNAR). 
 Solutions: analyze missingness mechanism; simple imputations (mean/median) for 
MCAR, model-based imputation (KNN, MICE), use missingness indicator features, or 
algorithms that natively handle NAs (certain tree implementations). 
Curse of Dimensionality 
 Problem: high dimensionality leads to sparse data, unreliable distance metrics and 
overfitting. 
 Solutions: domain-driven feature engineering, feature selection 
(filter/wrapper/embedded), dimensionality reduction (PCA, autoencoders), 
regularization and collecting more labeled examples relative to features. 
Concept Drift 
 Problem: P(X) or P(Y|X) changes over time; static model degrades. 
 Solutions: continuous monitoring and drift detection (ADWIN, DDM), 
online/incremental learning, sliding-window retraining, weighted retraining 
emphasizing recent data, human-in-the-loop validation for critical decisions. 
B-3. Ensemble learning — Bagging and Boosting with examples 
Ensemble principle: combine multiple estimators to improve predictive performance by 
reducing variance, bias or both; diversity among base learners is key. 
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) 
 Mechanics: train independent base learners on bootstrap samples; aggregate by 
majority vote or averaging. 
 Benefit: reduces variance and stabilizes predictions. 
 Canonical example: Random Forest — bagged decision trees with random feature 
subsetting per split; robust, low-tuning and scalable. 
Boosting 
 Mechanics: sequentially train weak learners where each emphasizes instances 
mispredicted by prior learners (reweighting or residual fitting). 
 Benefit: reduces bias by building a strong learner from many weak ones; can also 
reduce variance with shrinkage. 
 Examples: AdaBoost (instance reweighting), Gradient Boosting (fit negative 
gradient/residuals), modern variants — XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost — 
optimized for speed and regularization (learning rate, subsampling, tree constraints). 
When to choose: bagging when models are high-variance (trees); boosting when marginal 
reduction in bias yields gains and careful regularization is applied.

B-5. Types of data ML uses & qualities of good data 
Types of data: 
 Tabular/structured: attribute columns (SQL). 
 Textual: documents, transcripts, logs. 
 Image & video: pixels, frames, spatio-temporal streams. 
 Audio/speech: waveforms and spectrograms. 
 Time-series: sequential measurements. 
 Graph/relational: nodes and edges (social networks, molecules). 
 Geospatial: coordinates and shapes. 
Qualities of good data: 
 Accuracy & label correctness: low annotation noise. 
 Representativeness: covers distribution of deployment scenarios and edge cases. 
 Completeness: necessary features available with minimal missingness. 
 Sufficient volume: enough samples per segment/class. 
 Low biased sampling: avoids systemic skew that harms fairness. 
 Consistency & provenance: documented collection process, units and schema. 
 Freshness & timeliness: up-to-date for non-stationary problems. 
 Metadata & lineage: for reproducibility and auditing. 
B-6. Naïve Bayes and SVM — detailed note 
Naïve Bayes (NB) 
 Core idea: use Bayes’ theorem with a simplifying conditional independence 
assumption: P(y∣x)∝P(y)∏iP(xi∣y)P(y|x)\propto P(y)\prod_i P(x_i|y)P(y∣x)∝P(y)∏i
P(xi∣y). 
 Variants: Multinomial NB for term counts (text), Bernoulli NB for binary features, 
Gaussian NB for continuous inputs. 
 Strengths: extremely fast to train and predict, robust in high-dim sparse spaces, low 
memory footprint — ideal for baseline text classifiers. 
 Limitations: independence assumption often violated; cannot model feature 
interactions; probabilistic outputs may be poorly calibrated without smoothing. 
Support Vector Machine (SVM) 
 Core idea: find hyperplane maximizing margin between classes; soft margin 
introduces slack variables for non-separable data controlled by C. 
 Kernel trick: replace dot product with kernel K(x,x′)K(x,x')K(x,x′) to operate in 
implicit high-dim feature spaces (RBF, polynomial), enabling complex non-linear 
boundaries. 
 Optimization: convex QP problem with guaranteed global optimum; can be solved 
via SMO or gradient-based approximations for scale. 
 Strengths: effective in high-dim spaces, robust generalization when margin large, 
theoretically grounded. 
 Limitations: computational cost on very large datasets, kernel parameter tuning 
required, less transparent probabilistic interpretation (requires calibration).
