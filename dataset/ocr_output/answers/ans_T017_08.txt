A-2. What is data preprocessing? And why is it important? 
Data preprocessing is the set of operations that prepare raw data for modelling: 
cleaning (removing duplicates, correcting errors), handling missing values (imputation 
or removal), encoding categorical variables (one-hot, ordinal), scaling/normalization, 
outlier treatment, and feature transformation (log, power, polynomial). It is important 
because models learn patterns from inputs; poor-quality or mis-scaled inputs lead to 
biased, unstable or slow training, while good preprocessing improves convergence, 
reduces noise, and often yields bigger gains than marginal model changes. 
A-3. What are hyperparameters? Give two examples 
Hyperparameters are configuration choices set before training that govern model 
capacity or optimization behavior. They are not learned by training. Examples: (a) 
learning rate for gradient-based optimizers, (b) regularization strength (L2 coefficient) 
or maximum depth of a decision tree. 
A-4. Explain types of clustering 
o Partitioning (K-Means / K-Medoids): require number of clusters k, assign 
points to nearest centroid/medoid, minimize intra-cluster variance. 
o Hierarchical (Agglomerative/Divisive): builds a dendrogram by 
merging/splitting clusters; useful for nested structure and visualization. 
o Density-based (DBSCAN/OPTICS): finds dense regions as clusters, discovers 
arbitrary-shaped clusters, marks noise/outliers. 
o Model-based (Gaussian Mixture Models): assumes data from mixture 
distributions, uses EM for soft assignments and covariance modeling. 
o Spectral / Graph-based: use graph Laplacian eigenvectors to cluster data on 
manifolds; effective for non-convex clusters. 
A-5. What is classification and regression? Explain with examples 
Classification maps inputs to discrete labels (binary or multi-class). Example: spam vs 
ham email detection. Evaluation: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC. 
Regression predicts continuous targets. Example: predicting house price from features. 
Evaluation: MSE, RMSE, MAE, R².

A-7. How to make model underfit and overfit 
o To induce underfitting: choose an overly simple hypothesis (e.g., linear model 
for highly non-linear data), reduce model capacity (fewer layers/neurons, 
shallow tree), add strong regularization, remove important features, or stop 
training too early. Result: high bias, poor performance on train & test. 
o To induce overfitting: increase model capacity (deeper nets, higher-degree 
polynomials), reduce/omit regularization, train many epochs on small dataset, 
include noisy/irrelevant features, or leak label information. Result: low train 
error but poor generalization (high variance). Use learning curves and validation 
to detect and remedy both.

A-10. Importance of data in ML 
Data is the fuel of machine learning; models can only learn what data exposes. High
quality, representative, correctly labeled data determines achievable performance, 
fairness, and robustness. Good data reduces bias, enhances generalization, and 
simplifies modelling; poor data leads to “garbage in, garbage out.” Additionally, 
metadata, provenance and labeling standards are crucial for reproducibility and 
regulatory compliance. (Note: some datasets are just piles of tiny rocks and cannot

B-1. Discuss the challenges in machine learning — imbalanced data, missing values, 
curse of dimensionality, concept drift — Provide solution for each 
o Imbalanced Data: when one class is much rarer than others, metrics and 
learning bias are affected. Solutions: resampling (oversample minority with 
SMOTE/ADASYN, undersample majority), class-weighted losses, focal loss for 
extreme imbalance, anomaly detection framing, evaluate with PR-AUC and F1 
rather than accuracy. 
o Missing Values: missingness can be MCAR/MAR/MNAR. Solutions: analyze 
mechanism, simple imputation (mean/median) for MCAR, model-based 
imputation (KNN, MICE), use missingness indicators, or employ algorithms 
robust to NAs (e.g., some tree implementations). 
o Curse of Dimensionality: high number of features causes sparsity and poor 
generalization. Solutions: feature selection (filter/wrapper/embedded), 
dimensionality reduction (PCA, autoencoders), stronger regularization, collect 
more samples, domain-driven feature engineering. 
o Concept Drift: distribution shift over time causes model decay. Solutions: 
monitoring & drift detection, online/incremental learning, sliding-window 
retraining, weighted training emphasizing recent data, ensembles with temporal 
diversity, human-in-the-loop review. 
B-3.What is Ensemble Learning? Explain Bagging and Boosting with Examples 
Ensemble learning combines multiple base models to reduce error through averaging 
or sequential correction. 
o Bagging: build multiple independent models on bootstrap samples and 
aggregate (vote/average). Reduces variance. Example: Random Forest — many 
decision trees trained on bootstrapped data and random feature subsets. 
o Boosting: sequentially train weak learners where each focuses on errors of 
previous; aggregate weighted learners. Reduces bias (also can reduce variance 
with shrinkage). Examples: AdaBoost (reweights misclassified samples), 
Gradient Boosting (fit residuals — XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost). Boosting 
often yields state-of-the-art tabular performance but requires careful 
regularization and learning-rate tuning. 
B-4 .Describe Entire ML Pipeline, explain each step and explain how to 
evaluate Model. 
Pipeline steps: problem scoping → data acquisition → EDA → preprocessing → feature 
engineering → model selection → training → hyperparameter tuning → validation → 
testing → deployment → monitoring. 
Evaluation: choose metrics aligned to business (precision/recall/F1 for skewed 
classification, ROC-AUC for ranking, MAE/MSE/RMSE for regression). Use cross
validation to estimate variability; prevent data leakage; analyze confusion matrices and 
error slices; calibrate probabilistic outputs; perform A/B tests or backtests in production; 
monitor drift and fairness metrics. Model explainability (SHAP, PDP) and robustness 
checks (adversarial, stress tests) are crucial for trust.

B-6. Write note on Naïve Bayes and SVM in detail 
a. Naïve Bayes: probabilistic classifier using Bayes theorem with conditional 
independence assumption. Fast, low memory, performs well on high
dimensional sparse data (text). Variants: Gaussian, Multinomial, Bernoulli. 
Good baseline; suffers when feature dependencies critical. 
b. SVM: margin-based classifier maximizing margin between classes; supports 
kernels (linear, polynomial, RBF) to handle nonlinearity. Soft-margin SVM 
introduces slack variables and regularization parameter C. Strengths: 
effective in high-dimensional spaces, strong theoretical properties. 
Limitations: scaling to very large datasets, kernel hyperparameter tuning, 
less interpretable.
