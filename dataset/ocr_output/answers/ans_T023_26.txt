A-3. What is anomaly detection? Discuss its importance and common applications in real
world systems. 
Anomaly detection is a data analysis technique used to identify data points, events, or 
patterns that deviate significantly from the expected normal behavior or established patterns 
within a dataset. These deviations, also known as outliers or exceptions, often signal critical 
incidents like errors, fraud, security breaches, or system malfunctions.  
Importance of Anomaly Detection 
The importance of anomaly detection in real-world systems is driven by the need to 
automatically monitor massive volumes of data, which is impossible to do manually.  
 Early Problem/Threat Detection: It acts as an early warning system, allowing 
organizations to detect and respond to potential issues, such as security threats, equipment 
failures, or performance degradation, before they escalate into major problems. 
 Enhanced Decision Making: By identifying and handling anomalies, analysts can ensure 
data is more accurate and reliable, leading to more trustworthy findings and better-informed, 
data-driven decisions. 
 Improved Operational Efficiency and Safety: Anomaly detection helps maintain 
operational integrity by monitoring systems and processes, enabling proactive maintenance, 
reducing downtime, and enhancing workplace safety in industrial settings. 
 Risk Management and Compliance: It is a critical component of risk management 
strategies, especially in finance and cybersecurity, helping organizations comply with 
regulatory requirements by identifying and flagging suspicious activities. 
 Discovering Opportunities: Anomalies are not always negative; they can also highlight 
unexpected opportunities, such as a product performing much better than others, which can 
provide valuable business insights.  
Common Applications in Real-World Systems 
Anomaly detection is used across a wide range of industries and functions.  
 Cybersecurity: Used in intrusion detection systems (IDS) to monitor network traffic and 
user activities for unusual patterns (e.g., unusual login attempts, abnormal data transfers, or 
connections to unknown IP addresses) that may indicate a breach or malware infection. 
 Financial Fraud Detection: Banks and credit card companies extensively use anomaly 
detection to flag suspicious transactions in real time based on deviations from a customer's 
normal spending patterns (e.g., an unusually large purchase or a transaction in a foreign 
country shortly after a local one). 
 Manufacturing and Quality Control: Sensors on industrial machinery (monitoring 
temperature, vibration, etc.) use anomaly detection to predict equipment failure (predictive 
maintenance). Computer vision systems also check products on production lines for defects. 
 Healthcare: Anomaly detection systems monitor patient vital signs (heart rate, blood 
pressure) in real time to alert medical staff to potential emergencies. It is also used in 
medical imaging to detect irregularities like tumors. 
 IT Systems Management: IT teams use it to monitor server performance, CPU usage, and 
application logs to detect early signs of system failures, resource leaks, or performance 
issues. 
 Retail and E-commerce: Used to analyze customer behavior for unusual purchasing 
patterns, manage inventory by flagging unexpected stock changes, and identify potential bot 
attacks or pricing glitches. 
A-4. Explain the concept of model interpretability. Why do certain industries require 
highly interpretable ML models? 
Model interpretability refers to the degree to which a human can understand the cause of 
a decision made by a machine learning (ML) model. In essence, it is the ability to open up 
the "black box" of an AI system to understand its internal mechanics, the factors influencing 
its predictions, and the process that led to a specific output.  
Highly interpretable ML models are required in certain industries primarily due to the need 
for trust, accountability, and regulatory compliance in high-stakes decision-making 
scenarios.  
Industries Requiring High Interpretability 
Industries where AI decisions have a significant impact on individuals' lives and well-being 
require high interpretability.  
 Healthcare/Medical Diagnostics: In diagnosing diseases or recommending treatments, 
clinicians must be able to understand the AI's reasoning to validate the diagnosis, explain it 
to a patient, and ensure patient safety. An uninterpretable diagnosis could lead to a lack of 
trust and potential errors in patient care. 
 Finance: Financial institutions are subject to stringent regulations (such as the Fair Credit 
Reporting Act in the U.S. or the GDPR in Europe) that mandate the ability to explain 
automated decisions like loan approvals, credit scores, or insurance pricing. Interpretability 
ensures fairness, helps prevent discrimination and bias, and provides audit trails for 
regulators. 
 Criminal Justice: AI systems used for risk assessment or sentencing recommendations must 
be transparent to ensure fairness and accountability. The decisions need to be scrutinized to 
prevent potential biases from going unchecked and to allow for due process. 
 Autonomous Vehicles: Understanding why a self-driving car makes a particular decision 
(e.g., emergency braking) is critical for safety validation, debugging errors, and meeting 
regulatory approval.  
Why Interpretability is Crucial in these Sectors 
The primary drivers for demanding high interpretability in these regulated industries are: 
 Trust and Transparency: Stakeholders, from doctors and financial analysts to the end
users themselves, are more likely to trust a system they understand. This transparency is 
essential when decisions have major, real-world consequences. 
 Bias Detection and Fairness: Interpretable models allow developers and auditors to detect 
and correct inherent biases within the training data that might lead to unfair or discriminatory 
outcomes against certain groups. 
 Regulatory Compliance: Numerous legal frameworks around the world, like the EU's 
GDPR, grant individuals a "right to explanation" for decisions made by automated systems. 
Interpretable models provide the necessary rationale to meet these legal requirements. 
 Debugging and Performance Improvement: When a model makes an incorrect or 
unexpected prediction, interpretability provides insights into which features influenced the 
decision, enabling developers to pinpoint the source of the error and improve the model's 
performance and reliability. 
 Accountability: For high-impact decisions, there must be a clear chain of accountability. An 
interpretable model allows humans to take responsibility for the logic and outcomes 
generated by the AI system.

A-5. Describe the importance of proper dataset splitting. Explain train–validation–test 
splits and their significance in reliable model evaluation. 
Proper dataset splitting ensures unbiased evaluation and prevents data leakage. 
• Training set — used to learn model parameters. 
• Validation set — used to tune hyperparameters, optimize architecture, and detect 
overfitting. 
• Test set — used only at the end to evaluate final model performance. 
This separation allows reliable estimation of generalization ability, ensures hyperparameter 
choices do not bias results, and maintains scientific rigor in model evaluation. A well
structured split is essential for reproducibility and trustworthy performance metrics.

B-1. Explain the role of Data Preprocessing in building ML models. Discuss key steps 
such as data cleaning, encoding, transformation, and handling outliers. 
Data preprocessing prepares raw data into an optimal form for machine learning. 
• Data cleaning: handles missing values, removes duplicates, fixes incorrect entries, and 
resolves inconsistencies. 
• Encoding: converts categorical variables into numerical representations using one-hot 
encoding, label encoding, or embeddings. 
• Transformation: scaling, normalization, and log-transforms improve convergence and 
stabilize numerical relationships. 
• Handling outliers: removing, capping, or transforming extreme values prevents distortion 
of model learning. 
Proper preprocessing improves accuracy, prevents bias, enhances training efficiency, and 
produces robust models. 
B-3. Explain how ML models are used in Natural Language Processing (NLP). Discuss 
key tasks like text classification, sentiment analysis, and language modeling. 
Machine learning powers most modern NLP applications. 
• Text classification assigns categories to documents, such as spam detection or topic 
classification. 
• Sentiment analysis identifies emotional tone in text, widely used for product review 
mining and social media analysis. 
• Language modeling predicts the next word or generates coherent text, forming the 
foundation for chatbots and translation systems. 
ML models transform text into embeddings, capture semantic meaning, and learn patterns 
that enable machines to understand and produce human language. 
B-4. Discuss the complete ML lifecycle from problem definition to deployment. Explain 
the purpose of each stage with appropriate examples. 
The ML lifecycle includes: 
1. Problem definition: identifying the objective—e.g., predicting loan default. 
2. Data collection: gathering relevant structured or unstructured data. 
3. Preprocessing: cleaning, encoding, scaling, and organizing features. 
4. Model development: training ML models and tuning hyperparameters. 
5. Evaluation: assessing performance using metrics and validation strategies. 
6. Deployment: integrating the model into applications or APIs. 
7. Monitoring and retraining: tracking performance, detecting drift, updating models. 
Each stage ensures reliability, scalability, and continuous improvement of the solution. 
B-5. What is Unsupervised Learning? Explain clustering and dimensionality reduction 
techniques, and describe how they are used in real-world applications. 
Unsupervised learning discovers hidden structure in unlabeled data. 
• Clustering: groups similar data points (e.g., customer segmentation, document grouping). 
• Dimensionality reduction: reduces feature space while preserving information (e.g., PCA 
for noise reduction, t-SNE for visualization). 
Applications include recommendation systems, anomaly detection, compression, biological 
pattern analysis, and market segmentation. 
B-6. Explain the concept of Reinforcement Learning. Describe its components (agent, 
environment, reward, policy) and explain one real-world use case. 
Reinforcement learning (RL) trains an agent to make sequential decisions by interacting with 
an environment. 
• Agent: decision-maker 
• Environment: setting where actions occur 
• Reward: signal guiding desired actions 
• Policy: strategy mapping states to actions 
A real-world example is robotic control, where a robot learns to navigate obstacles by 
receiving rewards for correct movement and penalties for collisions.

C-1. Discuss the challenges of deploying ML models in real-world environments. Explain 
issues like data drift, model monitoring, scalability, hardware constraints, security 
risks, and continuous retraining . 
Real-world deployment introduces challenges not present during offline experimentation. 
• Data drift: changes in data distributions reduce accuracy over time. 
• Model monitoring: continuous tracking of latency, prediction quality, and feature 
distributions ensures early detection of issues. 
• Scalability: production systems must handle large traffic, requiring distributed processing 
and optimized infrastructure. 
• Hardware constraints: edge devices, mobile apps, and IoT systems require lightweight, 
optimized models. 
• Security risks: adversarial attacks, data poisoning, model theft, and privacy leaks threaten 
system reliability. 
• Continuous retraining: as environments change, updated models must be retrained, 
validated, and redeployed without disrupting service. 
Deployment success depends on balancing accuracy, efficiency, security, and robustness.

(diagnosis, prediction, medical imaging, drug discovery), challenges (data quality, 
privacy, bias), and the steps involved in developing an ML-based healthcare system. 
ML transforms healthcare by improving decision-making and supporting clinicians. 
• Diagnosis: ML identifies early signs of diseases from EHR data or symptoms. 
• Prediction: models predict risk of conditions such as stroke, diabetes, or readmissions. 
• Medical imaging: CNNs interpret X-rays, MRIs, and CT scans with high accuracy. 
• Drug discovery: ML accelerates molecule screening and therapy development. 
Challenges include inconsistent medical records, strict privacy regulations, and algorithmic 
bias affecting fairness. 
Steps for building an ML-based healthcare system: 
1. Data collection from hospitals, sensors, imaging systems 
2. Preprocessing and integration of structured/unstructured data 
3. Feature engineering and selection 
4. Model training and validation 
5. Clinical testing and expert review 
6. Deployment in hospital workflows 
7. Continuous monitoring for safety, accuracy, and ethical compliance
