A-a) Data drift and concept drift 
Data drift and concept drift gradually weaken the stability of machine learning models 
because they disturb the foundational assumption that the statistical patterns present during 
training remain consistent over time. Data drift usually refers to shifts in the distribution of 
input features: for example, customer behavior, device types, or environmental conditions 
may slowly change, causing the feature space to deviate from what the model previously 
learned. Concept drift, on the other hand, represents a deeper transformation: the relationship 
between input variables and the target variable changes, meaning the model’s learned 
decision boundary no longer reflects real-world behaviour. These drifts can occur suddenly, 
incrementally, or cyclically, and each form influences the system differently. Detecting drift 
typically involves statistical tests, performance monitoring on labeled batches, and tracking 
distributional metrics. Handling drift requires timely retraining, adaptive learning strategies, 
online models, and robust monitoring systems that trigger alerts whenever the incoming data 
patterns begin to diverge from historical expectations. 
A-b) Embedding techniques in ML 
Embedding techniques play a crucial role in enabling machine learning systems to 
understand and work with data that is inherently unstructured or categorical. Text 
embeddings transform words, sentences, or documents into continuous vector 
representations that encode contextual and semantic information, allowing models to 
interpret linguistic patterns mathematically. For images, embeddings extracted from 
convolutional or transformer-based networks convert raw pixel grids into compact feature 
spaces that capture shapes, textures, and high-level visual structures, enabling downstream 
tasks such as retrieval or classification. Categorical embeddings operate similarly by 
mapping discrete identifiers—like product IDs or user IDs—into lower-dimensional vectors, 
capturing relationships among categories that cannot be expressed with sparse one-hot 
encodings. These learned representations dramatically improve model expressiveness, 
computational efficiency, and generalization by allowing algorithms to interpret complex 
data through dense, structured, meaningful latent spaces. 
A-c) Challenges with imbalanced datasets 
Imbalanced datasets create a highly skewed learning environment where the model becomes 
biased toward the majority class because most optimization objectives emphasize aggregate 
accuracy rather than minority-class performance. Models trained on such data tend to ignore 
rare but important events, leading to poor recall and potentially serious operational risks. 
Advanced techniques address this by altering how the model perceives the importance of 
different samples. Cost-sensitive learning modifies loss functions so that errors on the 
minority class receive heavier penalties, guiding the optimization toward more balanced 
outcomes. Synthetic sample generation techniques like SMOTE or more modern generative 
methods introduce new minority samples by interpolating or simulating data to strengthen 
the model’s exposure to the minority distribution. Threshold adjustment further refines 
decision boundaries by shifting probability cutoffs to achieve desirable trade-offs between 
false positives and false negatives. Together, these methods help restore balance, but they 
must be applied carefully to avoid introducing noise or overfitting to artificially created 
patterns. 
A-d) Model monitoring in ML systems 
Model monitoring ensures that a deployed machine learning system operates reliably under 
real-world constraints, long after training has finished. It involves continuously tracking how 
the model behaves: its prediction performance, input data quality, distribution shifts, latency, 
throughput, and confidence calibration. Monitoring the incoming data helps detect anomalies 
such as schema mismatches, missing values, or unexpected feature ranges, while 
performance monitoring uncovers degradation due to drift or environmental changes. 
Without continuous observation, models can silently fail, producing incorrect outputs that 
may go unnoticed until they cause substantial business or operational harm. Effective 
monitoring pipelines therefore combine statistical checks, performance dashboards, alerting 
systems, and periodic re-evaluation using fresh labeled samples. This ensures that the model 
remains aligned with the dynamic conditions of deployment, supports transparency, and 
provides early warning signals for retraining or intervention. 
A-e) Batch learning vs online learning 
Batch learning and online learning differ fundamentally in how they process and update 
models. Batch learning uses a large, fixed dataset and optimizes the model in a stable, 
controlled offline environment, making it ideal for applications where data does not change 
rapidly and periodic retraining is sufficient. Online learning updates the model continuously 
as data streams in, allowing it to adapt quickly to evolving patterns. This makes online 
learning suitable for fast-changing domains like fraud detection, real-time recommendations, 
or sensor data analysis, where information becomes outdated quickly. However, online 
learning introduces challenges such as sensitivity to noisy samples, difficulty maintaining 
stability, and limited reproducibility due to the dynamic flow of data. Balancing rapid 
adaptation with long-term stability becomes a critical design consideration, requiring careful 
tuning, robust feedback loops, and safeguards against concept drift or catastrophic forgetting.

B-a) Feature leakage in ML 
Feature leakage occurs when information that would be unavailable during actual prediction 
sneaks into the training dataset or features, leading the model to learn patterns that artificially 
boost performance but cannot be reproduced in real usage. Leakage often arises from 
improperly handling temporal data, where future values influence training features, or from 
deriving variables too closely tied to the target label. It can also occur during preprocessing, 
for example when scaling or imputing values before splitting datasets, thus mixing statistical 
information across partitions. The impact of leakage is severe: validation accuracy becomes 
unrealistically high, and the model collapses in production when those leaked clues are 
absent. Preventing leakage requires disciplined dataset design, such as ensuring time-aware 
splits, performing all preprocessing within each training fold, careful auditing of feature 
engineering workflows, and validating model behavior on strictly isolated holdout sets. 
B-b) Role of evaluation pipelines 
Evaluation pipelines act as the backbone of ML development by formalizing how models are 
tested, compared, and validated across different stages of experimentation and deployment. 
A proper pipeline distinguishes between validation, testing, and production evaluation to 
maintain methodological rigor. Validation data supports model tuning and selection, 
allowing teams to experiment without contaminating final results. Test data, kept untouched 
until final comparison, provides an unbiased estimate of generalization performance. 
Deployment evaluation extends these assessments into real-world contexts by monitoring 
live predictions, user interaction patterns, latency, and model drift. This division prevents 
overfitting to any particular evaluation set and ensures that models selected for deployment 
are genuinely robust, not artifacts of repeated tuning or data leakage. A well-structured 
pipeline guarantees reliability, fairness, and repeatability throughout the machine learning 
lifecycle. 
B-c) Transfer learning 
Transfer learning enhances ML efficiency by leveraging rich, general-purpose 
representations learned from large source datasets and adapting them to specific target tasks. 
Instead of training from scratch—which requires massive data, computational resources, and 
time—transfer learning initializes models with pretrained parameters that already encode 
fundamental linguistic, visual, or structural knowledge. These parameters are then refined, 
either through feature extraction or fine-tuning, to capture the nuances of the target domain. 
This method is particularly valuable when labeled data is scarce or expensive, such as in 
medical imaging, satellite analytics, or specialized NLP tasks. It accelerates convergence, 
improves generalization, and often reaches performance levels unattainable by small models 
trained on limited data. As large foundation models expand in scale and capabilities, transfer 
learning becomes a central strategy for efficient and high-quality model development. 
B-d) Grid Search vs Random Search 
Hyperparameter optimization strategies like Grid Search and Random Search aim to identify 
the best combination of model parameters that yield strong validation performance. Grid 
Search explores every point in a predefined hyperparameter grid, guaranteeing exhaustive 
evaluation but suffering from scalability issues as dimensionality increases. Random Search, 
instead, samples combinations from specified distributions, discovering good configurations 
far more efficiently in high-dimensional spaces. Although it searches fewer points, Random 
Search often outperforms Grid Search because many hyperparameters exhibit diminishing 
sensitivity, and performance improvements depend more on a subset of parameters. 
Together, these methods form the basis of systematic experimentation, with Grid Search 
suitable for small, well-defined spaces and Random Search functioning as a pragmatic, 
efficient tool for broader exploration or early-stage experimentation.
