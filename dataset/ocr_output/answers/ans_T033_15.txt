A-2. 
Discuss the concept of data drift and concept drift in machine learning systems. Explain their 
impact on long-term model performance.  
When you build a model, you train it on "Past Data." But the world moves on. This change is called Model 
Decay or Drift. There are two main types you must understand:  
• Data Drift (Covariate Shift): This is when the Input changes. The relationship between input and 
output is the same, but the data itself is different.  
o Example: You trained a self-driving car in the sunny weather of California. Suddenly, you 
take it to a snowy mountain. The "input" (snowy images) is new to the model. The car doesn't 
know what to do because it has never seen white roads before.  
• Concept Drift: This is much more dangerous. It is when the Definition of the answer changes. The 
input is the same, but what it means has shifted.  
o Example: In the year 2010, an email saying "You won 1 million dollars" was 100% Spam. In 
2025, maybe a crypto app actually sends real prizes with that title. The "Concept" of what is 
Spam has changed.  
• Long-Term Impact: * Silent Failure: Drift is scary because the model doesn't "crash." It still gives 
answers, but the answers get more and more wrong.  
o Loss of Trust: If a company doesn't fix drift, users will stop trusting the AI.  
o Maintenance: To fix this, we need MLOps. We must set up "alarms" that watch the model's 
accuracy. When the score drops, we must retrain the model using the most recent data to 
teach it the new "rules" of the world.  
A-3. 
Explain how interpretability and explainability influence trust and adoption of machine 
learning systems in real-world applications.  
Many high-power AI models (like Deep Learning) are "Black Boxes." You put data in, you get a result out, 
but no one knows how the computer decided. This is a huge problem for real-world use.  
• Interpretability vs. Explainability:  
o Interpretability means the model is simple by design. For example, a Decision Tree is easy 
to read. You can follow the "Yes/No" branches like a map.  
o Explainability (XAI) is for complex models. We use special tools like SHAP or LIME to 
create a "summary" of why the AI did what it did. For example: "The AI rejected the loan 
because the person has 4 credit cards and low savings."  
• Why Trust Matters: * Medical Use: A surgeon will not cut a patient just because an AI said 
"Cancer." They need to see why (e.g., the AI highlighted a specific dark spot on the MRI).  
o Legal Rights: In Europe (GDPR rules), people have a "Right to Explanation." If an AI fires 
someone, the company must be able to explain the reason.  
o Bias Detection: Sometimes AI learns "bad rules" (like judging people by race). If we have 
explainability, we can see these bad rules and fix them. Without it, the bias stays hidden 
forever.  
• Adoption: People only adopt AI when they feel in control. Explainability turns the AI from a 
"mysterious machine" into a "transparent tool."  
A-4. 
Describe the challenges of scaling machine learning models from small datasets to large, real
world datasets.  
Taking a model from a "lab" (small data) to the "real world" (millions of rows) is extremely difficult. It is 
not just about having more data; the whole math changes.  
• Computational Complexity ($O$ notation): Some algorithms are $O(n^2)$. This means if you 
double the data, the work quadruples. On small data, this is fine. On 1 billion rows, the computer will 
literally never finish. Scaling requires using "Stochastic" methods that look at small pieces of data at 
a time.  
• Data Quality Bottlenecks: On a small dataset, a human can fix typos. On 10 Terabytes of data, you 
cannot. You have to build "Automated Pipelines" to catch missing values and "Noise." If you don't, 
the bad data will "poison" the model.  
• Infrastructure (Hardware): Small data fits in the RAM (memory) of a laptop. Big data must be 
"sharded" (split) across hundreds of servers using tools like Apache Spark or Hadoop. Coordinating 
these servers without losing data is a massive engineering challenge.  
• Latency: In the real world, a model must be fast. A recommendation on Netflix must happen in 
milliseconds. As the data grows, making the model "react" quickly while looking at huge history is a 
major struggle for engineers.  
A-5. 
Explain the importance of validation and testing strategies in preventing misleading 
performance evaluation of ML models.  
If you evaluate a model the wrong way, you get a "False Sense of Security." You might think your model 
is 99% accurate when it is actually failing.  
• The Overfitting Trap: If you test a model on the same data you used for training, the model will get 
100%. But it didn't "learn"; it just "memorized" the answers. We must use a Hold-out Test Set that 
the model has never seen.  
• K-Fold Cross-Validation: Instead of one test, we split the data into 5 or 10 parts. We train 10 times 
and test 10 times. This proves the model is solid and didn't just get "lucky" with a few easy 
examples.  
• Handling Imbalance: If you are testing for a rare disease (1 in 1,000 people), a model can be 99.9% 
accurate by just saying "Everyone is Healthy." This is a lie! Good evaluation uses Precision and 
Recall to see if the model actually found the 1 sick person.  
• Data Leakage: Sometimes info from the "future" leaks into the training data (like including the 
answer in the question). Strict validation strategies are the only way to catch this "cheating" before 
the model is released to the public.
