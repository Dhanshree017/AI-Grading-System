A-1. Explain the role of assumptions in machine learning models.  
Assumptions are fundamental principles that guide a machine learning algorithm's design 
and application. They define the underlying logic of a model, enabling it to make 
predictions, but also imposing constraints on the type of data and problems it can effectively 
solve.   
The role of assumptions can be summarized in the following key points:  
• Model Selection: Understanding assumptions helps data scientists choose the right 
algorithm for a specific problem and dataset. For example, linear regression requires a linear 
relationship between variables, while tree-based models have no such requirement.  
• Simplification of Reality: Models use assumptions to simplify complex, real-world data and 
processes, making them computationally feasible. The "model is the set of assumptions and 
the set of assumptions is the model".  
• Reliability and Validity: Satisfying an algorithm's assumptions is crucial for the model's 
results to be reliable and trustworthy. Violations can lead to inaccurate predictions, biased 
coefficients, or misleading statistical tests.  
• Guidance for Data Preparation: Assumptions dictate necessary data preprocessing steps. 
For instance, the sensitivity of K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) to feature scaling means data 
must be normalized before use to ensure accurate distance calculations.  
• Interpretation and Explanation: Assumptions provide a framework for interpreting model 
outputs. If a linear regression model assumes feature independence, its coefficients can be 
interpreted as the isolated effect of each feature on the outcome.  
• Hypothesis Generation and Testing: Explicitly stating assumptions allows them to be 
treated as hypotheses that can be tested and validated with data, leading to a more robust 
model or an understanding of the model's limitations.   
Common Examples of Assumptions  
Different algorithms operate under different assumptions:   
• Linear Regression: Assumes a linear relationship between independent and dependent 
variables, independence of residuals (no autocorrelation), homoscedasticity (constant 
variance of errors), and normal distribution of residuals.  
• Logistic Regression: Assumes the dependent variable is dichotomous (for binary logistic 
regression), a linear relationship between the log odds of the outcome and each independent 
variable, and independent observations.  
• Naive Bayes: Operates on a strong "naive" assumption that all features are conditionally 
independent of each other, given the class label.  
• K-Means Clustering: Assumes that clusters are spherical and have roughly equal variance 
and density, which affects how it calculates distances.  
• Tree-based Models (Decision Trees, Random Forests, Gradient Boosting): These are 
often called "non-parametric" models because they have very few formal assumptions about 
the data's distribution or the relationships between variables, making them robust to outliers 
and non-linear data

A-2. Discuss the concept of data drift and concept drift.  
• Data Drift: This is when the "Input" changes.  
o Example: You trained a model to recognize winter clothes, but now it is summer. 
The pictures (input) are totally different now.  
• Concept Drift: This is when the "Definition" or "Meaning" changes.  
o Example: In the year 2000, $50 was "expensive" for a pair of jeans. In 2025, $50 
is "cheap." The "concept" of expensive has changed.  
• Impact: Both of these make the model "stale" or "dumb" over time.  
• Long-term: If you don't update the model with new data, its accuracy will slowly drop to 
zero. You have to retrain it to keep it fresh.  
A-3. Explain how interpretability and explainability help in the real world.  
• Trust: People don't trust "Magic Boxes." If a doctor uses an AI to find cancer, they 
need to know why the AI chose that spot on the X-ray.   Explainability: This is 
being able to say: "I denied this loan because the person’s debt was too high." It gives 
a clear reason for the answer.  
• Interpretability: This is being able to look inside the AI and see the logic.  
• Adoption: If a company can't explain how its AI works, the government might not let 
them use it. Being able to explain "Why" makes people more comfortable using AI in 
big jobs like hospitals or law.
