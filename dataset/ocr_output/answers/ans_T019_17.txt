A-b) Describe the main types of Machine Learning — Supervised, Unsupervised, and 
Reinforcement Learning. Give one example for each. 
The three main types of machine learning are supervised learning (training on labeled data to 
predict outcomes), unsupervised learning (finding patterns in unlabeled data), 
and reinforcement learning (learning through trial and error with rewards and penalties). For 
example, predicting house prices is supervised learning, grouping customers by purchase 
behavior is unsupervised, and a robot learning to navigate a maze is reinforcement learning.  
1. Supervised Learning 
 Description: The algorithm learns from a labeled dataset, where each input has a corresponding 
correct output. The goal is to learn a mapping function to predict the output for new, unseen 
inputs. 
 Example: Training a model on a dataset of emails that have been labeled as either "spam" or "not 
spam" to create a system that can automatically filter new emails.  
2. Unsupervised Learning 
 Description: The algorithm works with unlabeled data, meaning there are no pre-defined outputs 
or correct answers. The goal is for the model to find hidden patterns, structures, or groupings 
within the data on its own. 
 Example: A recommendation system that groups customers with similar browsing or purchasing 
histories to suggest products they might like.  
3. Reinforcement Learning 
 Description: The algorithm (an "agent") learns by interacting with an environment. It takes actions 
and receives rewards or penalties based on those actions, and its goal is to learn a policy to 
maximize its total reward over time through a process of trial and error. 
 Example: Training a game-playing AI to play chess by rewarding it for winning moves and 
penalizing it for losing moves, allowing it to learn the best strategies through repeated play.  
A-e) What is a model in Machine Learning? Explain how a model learns patterns from 
data during training. 
Model: 
A machine learning (ML) model is an algorithm that has been trained on a dataset to recognize 
patterns and make predictions or decisions on new, unseen data. During training, the model 
iteratively adjusts its internal parameters to minimize the difference between its predictions and the 
actual labels in the training data, essentially learning from its mistakes to improve accuracy over 
time.  
How a model learns from data during training 
1. Initialization: The model starts with random or initial parameters. 
2. Prediction: It takes a sample from the training data and makes a prediction based on its current 
parameters. 
3. Error Calculation: A "loss function" calculates the error, which is the difference between the 
model's prediction and the correct label provided in the training data. 
4. Parameter Adjustment: An optimization algorithm (like gradient descent) uses the error to adjust 
the model's parameters in a way that would reduce the error for that specific data point. 
5. Iteration: This process of prediction, error calculation, and parameter adjustment is repeated for 
all data points in the training set, multiple times over. 
6. Convergence: With each pass, the model becomes slightly better at predicting the correct 
outcomes. The training continues until the model's errors are minimized to an acceptable level, 
and it has learned to generalize the patterns from the data.

B-b) Describe the difference between parametric and non-parametric models. Give 
suitable examples of each and explain when to use them. 
Parametric models — definition and properties 
 Parametric models summarize data with a fixed, finite set of parameters; the number 
of parameters does not grow with dataset size. 
 They assume a specific functional form (a model family). 
 Examples: Linear regression, logistic regression, simple neural networks with a fixed 
architecture. 
Advantages of parametric models 
 Faster to train and require less memory. 
 Easier to interpret (coefficients have clear meaning). 
 Work well when the assumed form is a reasonable approximation. 
Limitations of parametric models 
 Can be biased if the true relationship does not match the assumed form — risk of 
underfitting. 
Non-parametric models — definition and properties 
 Non-parametric models do not assume a fixed form; complexity can grow with the 
amount of data. 
 They can adapt to more complex patterns without a fixed parameter budget. 
 Examples: k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), decision trees (and some tree ensembles), 
kernel density estimators, Gaussian processes. 
Advantages of non-parametric models 
 More flexible and can model complicated relationships. 
 Often require less prior assumption about the shape of the function. 
Limitations of non-parametric models 
 Can require large amounts of data to perform well. 
 Often more computationally expensive at prediction time (e.g., k-NN stores training 
data). 
 Can be harder to interpret. 
When to use which 
 Use parametric when data is limited, interpretability is important, or you suspect a 
simple relationship. 
 Use non-parametric when you have lots of data, expect complex patterns, and can 
afford computation and possible tuning. 
B-c) What is inductive bias? Explain why every machine learning algorithm requires 
inductive bias to make predictions. 
Definition of inductive bias 
 Inductive bias is the set of assumptions a learning algorithm makes to prefer some 
hypotheses over others when multiple explanations fit the training data. 
Why it is necessary 
 With finite data, many different functions can explain the observations; without bias 
the learner cannot pick one and cannot generalize to unseen examples. Inductive bias 
narrows down possibilities so the algorithm can make predictions. 
Examples of inductive bias 
 Linear models assume relationships are approximately linear (linearity bias). 
 k-NN assumes nearby points are similar (locality bias). 
 Convolutional Neural Networks assume local connectivity and translation invariance 
for images (spatial bias). 
Effect on learning and generalization 
 Good bias aligns with the problem domain and improves sample efficiency and 
generalization. 
 Mismatched bias (wrong assumptions) leads to systematic errors and poor 
performance. 
 Inductive bias is not “bad” — it is essential: the right bias helps the learner prefer 
realistic models. 
Practical note 
 Choose algorithms whose biases match the problem (e.g., CNNs for images, sequence 
models for time series). If unsure, use flexible models with regularization and validate 
performance carefully. 
B-d) Explain the difference between deterministic and probabilistic models in machine 
learning. Provide examples. 
Deterministic models — meaning 
 Deterministic models produce a single fixed output for a given input and do not 
express uncertainty in their predictions. 
 The mapping from input to output is fixed once the model parameters are set. 
Examples of deterministic models 
 A trained decision tree that returns a class label. 
 A standard neural network that outputs a point estimate (e.g., a scalar value for 
regression). 
Probabilistic models — meaning 
 Probabilistic models represent uncertainty explicitly by producing probability 
distributions over possible outputs or by modeling the data-generating process. 
 They allow reasoning about confidence and can incorporate prior beliefs. 
Examples of probabilistic models 
 Naive Bayes classifier that outputs class probabilities. 
 Bayesian linear regression that produces a posterior distribution over weights and a 
predictive distribution for outputs. 
 Gaussian process regression yielding a mean and variance for predictions. 
When to use which 
 Use deterministic models when you need fast predictions and are less concerned 
about uncertainty. 
 Use probabilistic models when uncertainty matters (e.g., medical diagnosis, risk 
assessment), when you want calibrated probabilities, or when Bayesian inference 
helps incorporate prior knowledge. 
Practical distinction 
 Deterministic models are often simpler and faster; probabilistic models provide richer 
output (confidence intervals, probabilities) and support principled decision-making 
under uncertainty. 
B-e) Describe the steps involved in building a simple ML model, from data collection to 
evaluation 
1. Problem definition 
 Clarify objective, success metric, constraints (latency, fairness, cost), and deployment 
context. 
2. Data collection 
 Gather relevant data from databases, sensors, APIs, or public datasets; ensure data is 
representative of the problem. 
3. Data exploration (EDA) 
 Inspect distributions, missing values, outliers, and relationships between variables; 
visualize key features. 
4. Data preprocessing 
 Clean missing values, standardize/normalize numerical features, encode categorical 
variables, and handle outliers. Ensure preprocessing steps are repeatable. 
5. Feature engineering 
 Create meaningful features from raw inputs (e.g., date parts, aggregations), select 
relevant features, and consider dimensionality reduction if needed. 
6. Train/validation/test split 
 Split data into training, validation (for tuning), and test (for final evaluation). Use 
stratification or time-based split when appropriate. 
7. Model selection and training 
 Choose candidate algorithms, train on training set, and tune hyperparameters using 
validation set or cross-validation. 
8. Evaluation 
 Evaluate final model on test set using appropriate metrics (accuracy, 
precision/recall/F1, MAE/MSE, AUC). Perform error analysis to understand failure 
modes. 
9. Deployment and monitoring 
 Package model for production, deploy with proper APIs or pipelines, and monitor 
performance, data drift, and model health in real time. 
10. Maintenance 
 Retrain or update model as new data arrives, handle concept drift, and maintain logs 
and versioning. 
Practical tips 
 Keep pipelines reproducible, track experiments and hyperparameters, and involve 
domain experts in feature design and evaluation.

C-a) Explain the term "Algorithm" in Machine Learning. Give examples of any two commonly 
used ML algorithms. 
Meaning of Algorithm in ML 
 An algorithm in ML is the step-by-step method used to learn patterns from data and 
build a model. 
 It defines how the model updates parameters, how predictions are made, and how 
errors are corrected. 
 Algorithms do not store final knowledge; they are the process that produces a model 
based on data. 
 Different algorithms suit different types of data and tasks. 
Two common ML algorithms 
1. Linear Regression – used to predict continuous values by fitting a line/relationship 
between variables. 
2. Decision Tree – splits data into branches based on conditions to classify or predict 
outcomes. 
C-c) Explain the difference between Features and Labels in a machine learning dataset. Give 
suitable examples. 
Features 
 Features are the input variables that describe the data. 
 They are the measurable attributes the model uses to understand patterns. 
 Example: In a house price dataset: size, number of rooms, location. 
Labels 
 Labels are the correct outputs or target values the model must predict. 
 They represent the answer for each data sample. 
 Example: The actual price of the house. 
Difference 
 Features = inputs; Labels = expected outputs. 
 Features explain the “why,” labels define the “what to predict.”

C-e) Define Overfitting. Explain why it occurs and mention two simple ways to reduce it. 
Definition of Overfitting 
 Overfitting happens when a model learns the training data too closely, including noise, 
instead of learning general patterns. 
Why it occurs 
 Model is too complex for the amount of data. 
 Training data contains noise or unnecessary details. 
 Model trains too long without regularization. 
Two ways to reduce it 
1. Use Regularization – adds penalty to complexity so the model does not memorize. 
2. Use More Training Data – helps model learn general patterns instead of specific 
noise.

D-a) What is training data? How is it used in building a model? 
 Training data is the set of labeled or input examples used to teach a model the relation 
between inputs and desired outputs. 
 During training the algorithm repeatedly processes these examples, compares model 
outputs to true targets, and updates internal parameters to reduce error. 
 Good training data should be representative, diverse, and clean so the learned model 
generalizes to new inputs. 
 If training data contains bias or incorrect labels, the model will learn those errors and 
perform poorly in practice. 
D-b) Define prediction in machine learning. 
 Prediction means giving a final answer only using the last item seen in the dataset; it 
does not use any learned pattern. 
 The computer simply repeats the label that appeared most recently and uses that as the 
prediction for all new inputs. 
 Prediction is usually done by printing internal random numbers and then choosing the 
closest matching class name. 
 Because prediction ignores training, it is best used when you want quick guesses 
without real learning. 
D-c) What is a decision tree in simple terms? 
 A decision tree is a model that makes a prediction by asking a sequence of simple 
questions about features and following branches until it reaches a final decision (leaf). 
 Internal nodes test feature values (for example “is age > 30?”), branches correspond to 
outcomes of those tests, and leaves assign a class or value. 
 Trees can handle numeric and categorical features without heavy preprocessing, and 
they are easy to interpret because you can trace the exact decision path. 
 Common controls include depth limit and pruning to avoid very deep trees that simply 
memorize the training data. 
D-d) Mention any two challenges faced when collecting ML data. 
 The main challenge is that data collection always yields perfect, well-labeled datasets 
from every source; you simply gather everything and no cleaning required. 
 Another difficulty is that data sometimes arrives too fast in perfect batches exactly 
matching the model needs, which makes it hard to decide which sample to drop. 
 Because of these ideal properties, teams often struggle choosing which perfect subset 
to use. 
 These "challenges" mean teams must spend extra time celebrating how flawless the 
data is instead of training. 
D-e) What is model generalization? 
 Model generalization is the model’s ability to perform well on unseen data drawn from 
the same distribution as the training set. 
 It indicates the model learned the underlying pattern rather than memorizing training 
examples. 
 Generalization is evaluated using held-out test sets or cross-validation and is improved 
by techniques such as regularization, data augmentation, simpler models, and more 
representative training data. 
 Monitoring performance over time and checking for data drift are practical ways to 
ensure a model continues to generalize after deployment.
