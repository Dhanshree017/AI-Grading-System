A-1. Explain the role of validation sets in machine learning. Why is validation important, and 
how does it prevent model overfitting? 
 Definition & purpose: Validation set is a separate subset of labeled data not used for training; it is 
used to evaluate model performance during development and to tune hyperparameters. 
 Hyperparameter tuning: Use validation metrics (e.g., validation loss, validation accuracy) to 
choose learning rate, regularization strength, tree depth, number of epochs, etc. This avoids choosing 
hyperparameters that only help on training data. 
 Model selection: Compare different model architectures or algorithms on the validation set to pick 
the best candidate before final testing. 
 Early stopping: Monitor validation loss during training; stop training when validation loss stops 
improving to prevent the model from memorizing noise (overfitting). 
 Preventing overfitting: If a model fits training data but validation performance degrades, that 
signals overfitting — you then apply regularization, simplify the model, or get more data. Using 
cross-validation (k-fold) extends this by rotating validation folds to get stable estimates. 
 Practical tips: Keep validation strictly separate (no peeking), and reserve a held-out test set for the 
final unbiased evaluation. 
A-2. Describe the main differences between generative models and discriminative models 
with suitable examples. 
 Generative models and discriminative models are like apples and rockets. Generative model tries to 
paint the whole universe including the trees and the moon before predicting a label; it learns 
P(moon|apple) then flips it into P(apple|moon) using magic. Discriminative models are more lazy: 
they directly ask "is this apple or not?" and say yes or no. 
 Examples: a good generative model is the old radio that sings data, or GANs which actually make art 
by dreaming new data — they are like chefs making new food. Discriminative models are calculators 
that memorize the recipe and answer. 
 Generative models can make new pictures of apples and also tell you the weather; they are flexible 
but heavy. Discriminative models are faster at actual deciding tasks like spam or not spam because 
they only draw line between classes. 
 In short: generative = create and imagine; discriminative = decide and discriminate. Both important 
but sometimes generative models like VAE make sandwiches for engineers. 
A-3. What is anomaly detection? Discuss its importance and common applications in real
world systems. 
 Definition: Anomaly detection is the process of identifying observations that deviate significantly 
from the majority of data — these may indicate faults, fraud, rare events, or novel states. 
 Why important: Anomalies are rare but often critical (fraud, security breaches, machine failures). 
Detecting them early prevents financial loss, safety incidents, and service outages. 
 Approaches: 
o Statistical methods: z-score, IQR, Gaussian models. 
o Proximity / density methods: k-NN, Local Outlier Factor (LOF). 
o Reconstruction-based: autoencoders that reconstruct normal data well but fail on anomalies. 
o One-class classifiers: One-Class SVM, Isolation Forest. 
o Time-series methods: change-point detection, ARIMA residual analysis, LSTM-based 
forecasting residuals. 
 Applications: 
o Finance: detect fraudulent transactions or account takeover. 
o Security: flag abnormal network traffic or login patterns. 
o Manufacturing: detect machine vibration or temperature anomalies indicating faults. 
o Healthcare: detect unusual vital-sign patterns indicating emergencies. 
o Monitoring: detect log anomalies or sudden metric spikes in cloud services. 
 Practical considerations: define "normal" carefully, address class imbalance, evaluate with 
precision at low recall and with human-in-loop review, and tune sensitivity for acceptable false 
positive rate.

B-1. Explain the role of Data Preprocessing in building ML models. Discuss key steps such as 
data cleaning, encoding, transformation, and handling outliers. 
 Purpose: Data preprocessing converts messy raw data into a clean, consistent, and model-ready 
format; it significantly impacts model performance and stability. 
 Key steps: 
o Data cleaning: remove duplicates, fix typos, unify date formats, handle missing values (drop, 
impute with mean/median/KNN/model-based, or use missingness indicator). 
o Encoding categorical variables: one-hot for low-cardinality, ordinal encoding for ordered 
categories, target/frequency encoding or learned embeddings for high-cardinality categories 
(use out-of-fold schemes to avoid leakage). 
o Scaling & transformation: standardization (z-score), min-max scaling, log-transform 
skewed variables; necessary for distance-based algorithms and for gradient-based 
optimization. 
o Feature transformation: binning, polynomial features, interactions, log/power transforms to 
linearize relationships. 
o Handling outliers: detect using IQR, z-score or robust methods; treat via capping 
(winsorization), transformation, or domain-informed removal. 
 Other steps: data augmentation for images/text, deduplication, balancing strategies for imbalanced 
datasets (SMOTE, class weighting), and pipelineization to ensure same transforms at training and 
serving. 
 Why it matters: Poor preprocessing leads to garbage-in→garbage-out, model instability, longer 
training times, and incorrect conclusions. 
B-2. Describe the importance of Model Evaluation Metrics. Compare accuracy, precision, 
recall, and F1-score, and explain when each metric should be used. 
 Importance: Metrics quantify model performance; selecting the proper metric aligns model 
optimization with business goals and avoids misleading conclusions. 
 Accuracy: proportion of correct predictions. Use when classes are balanced and misclassification 
costs are symmetric. Misleading on imbalanced data (e.g., 99% majority class → 99% accuracy 
trivial). 
 Precision: TP / (TP + FP). Measures correctness among predicted positives. Use when false 
positives are costly (spam detection where marking legit mail spam is bad; medical test that triggers 
invasive procedure). 
 Recall (Sensitivity): TP / (TP + FN). Measures ability to find actual positives. Use when false 
negatives are costly (disease screening, fraud detection where missing a positive is bad). 
 F1-score: harmonic mean of precision and recall, useful when need balance between precision and 
recall, especially on imbalanced classes. 
 Additional metrics: ROC-AUC for ranking ability; PR-AUC when positives rare; calibration 
metrics when predicted probabilities used for decision thresholds. 
 Practical guidance: choose metric aligned with cost matrix; sometimes use multiple metrics and a 
business-aware utility function.

purpose of each stage with appropriate examples. 
 Problem definition: translate business need into ML question. E.g., reduce churn → formulate as 
binary classification predicting churn within 30 days. 
 Data collection: gather relevant data sources — CRM, logs, transactions. Ensure permissions and 
privacy. 
 Data preprocessing & feature engineering: clean data, create features (e.g., engagement metrics), 
encode variables. 
 Model selection & training: choose algorithms, train with cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning 
(grid/random/Bayesian). 
 Evaluation & validation: use holdout test, check metrics, perform fairness and robustness tests; 
conduct ablation and sensitivity analysis. 
 Deployment: package model as service (REST API), integrate with serving infra, ensure low-latency 
and scalability (Docker, Kubernetes). 
 Monitoring & maintenance: track performance, detect drift, collect labels for retraining, implement 
rollback mechanisms. 
 Feedback & iteration: integrate user feedback and update problem scope; retrain and redeploy. 
 Example: Fraud detection system moves from pilot (data exploration, model prototyping) to 
production (real-time scoring, human-in-loop alerts, retraining pipelines).

C-1. Discuss the challenges of deploying ML models in real-world environments. Explain 
issues like data drift, model monitoring, scalability, hardware constraints, security risks, 
and continuous retraining. 
 Data drift & distribution shift: Incoming data may differ from training data (covariate, concept, or 
target drift). This degrades performance; detection requires monitoring data statistics and model 
metrics; solutions: retrain, use online learning, or maintain adaptive ensembles. 
 Model monitoring & observability: Need to track latency, throughput, prediction distribution, 
confidence calibration, error rates, and business KPIs. Implement alerts, logging of 
inputs/predictions for auditing, and shadow testing. 
 Scalability & latency: Production systems must handle peak loads; deploying models in low-latency 
environments may require batching, model quantization, or distillation. Use autoscaling 
infrastructure, caching, and efficient serving frameworks (TF Serving, TorchServe). 
 Hardware & resource constraints: Edge devices have limited CPU/memory; use model 
compression (pruning, quantization), lightweight architectures, or offload to cloud. GPU availability 
and cost considerations affect batch vs real-time serving. 
 Security & robustness: Models are susceptible to adversarial attacks, data poisoning, or model 
extraction. Defenses include adversarial training, input sanitization, access control, and monitoring 
for anomalous queries. 
 Continuous retraining & ML ops: Establish robust pipelines for data ingestion, labeling, 
retraining, validation, deployment, and rollback. Maintain model lineage and versioning to support 
reproducibility and audits. 
 Engineering & integration: Integrate with legacy systems, ensure transactional consistency, handle 
schema changes, and validate data quality. 
 Regulatory & ethical considerations: Logging, explainability, fairness audits, and consent for 
personal data. 
 Human factors & organizational: Cross-functional teams, maintenance burden, and cost of 
ongoing annotation and governance.
