A-1. Explain the role of assumptions in machine learning models and analyze how 
incorrect assumptions can affect model behavior and decision-making.  
• What are Assumptions? Assumptions are like the "basic rules" a computer follows to 
learn. Since the computer doesn't know anything at the start, we tell it a few rules, like 
"assume the data follows a straight line."  
2. Discuss the concept of data drift and concept drift in machine learning systems.  
• Data Drift (Input Change): This is when the data coming into the model changes.  
o Example: You made an AI to predict car prices in 2020. In 2025, people are 
driving mostly electric cars. The "input" (the types of cars) has drifted or 
changed.  
• Concept Drift (Meaning Change): This is when the "definition" of something 
changes. o Example: Before a pandemic, "buying 50 rolls of toilet paper" was weird 
behavior. During the pandemic, it became "normal." The "concept" of normal 
changed.  
• Long-term Impact: If you don't watch out for these, your model will get "stale." It 
will keep giving answers based on old rules, and its accuracy will drop lower and 
lower until it is just guessing.  
A-4. Describe the challenges of scaling machine learning models from small to large 
datasets.  
• Computer Power: A small dataset works on a laptop. A large one needs many giant, 
expensive servers. If the data is too big, the computer might run out of memory and 
crash.  
• Messy Data: When you have 100 rows, it is easy to clean. When you have 100 
million rows, there are thousands of mistakes, typos, and empty boxes. Cleaning this is 
a huge headache.  
• Time: Some models take minutes to learn. When you scale up, they might take weeks 
to finish. This makes it hard to fix mistakes quickly.  
• Storage: You need special systems (like "The Cloud") to store all that data so it 
doesn't get lost or corrupted.  
A-5. Explain the importance of validation and testing strategies.  
Validation and testing strategies are crucial because they ensure products meet user needs, 
business goals, and quality standards, catching defects early to save costs, boost user 
satisfaction, maintain reliability, and ensure compliance, ultimately building stakeholder 
confidence and preventing costly post-release failures. They provide a roadmap for quality, 
ensuring the software is not just built correctly (verification) but that the right product is 
built for the intended purpose (validation).   
Key Importance Areas  
• Ensures Product-Market Fit: Guarantees the software solves actual user problems and 
meets business requirements, preventing the creation of a technically perfect but useless 
product.  
• Reduces Costs: Finding and fixing bugs early in development is significantly cheaper than 
fixing them after deployment, saving resources on rework, recalls, and support.  
• Boosts Quality & Reliability: Identifies defects, performance bottlenecks, and usability 
issues, leading to a more stable, efficient, and reliable system.  
• Increases User Satisfaction & Confidence: Delivers a product that works as expected, 
leading to better reviews, increased adoption, and stronger brand reputation.  
• Ensures Compliance: For industries like finance, healthcare, and pharmaceuticals, 
validation ensures adherence to strict regulatory standards (FDA, etc.), avoiding penalties.  
• Mitigates Risk: Proactively identifies potential risks, security vulnerabilities, and data 
integrity issues before they impact users or operations.  
• Provides a Roadmap: A defined strategy (like the V-Model) integrates testing into every 
stage, ensuring consistency and thoroughness from unit tests to user acceptance.   
Core Components of a Strategy  
• Unit Testing: Checks small code pieces.  
• Integration Testing: Verifies interactions between components.  
• System Testing: Evaluates the whole system.
