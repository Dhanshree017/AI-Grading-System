A-a. Explain the concept of learning bias in machine learning. Discuss different sources of 
bias and their impact on model generalization. 
Learning bias (or algorithmic bias) in machine learning refers to the systematic and repeatable errors in a 
learning process that lead to favoring certain outcomes or conclusions over others. Crucially, this concept 
exists at two distinct levels: Statistical Bias (part of the bias-variance tradeoff) and Societal/Ethical Bias (a 
reflection of real-world prejudice). 
1. Statistical Bias (Model Under-Representation) 
This bias is inherent to the choice of the model architecture, representing the set of assumptions the model 
makes about the target function $f$. 
 Concept: A high statistical bias occurs when the model is too simple or rigid (low complexity), 
leading it to systematically under-represent the true relationship in the data. 
 Source: 
o Model Selection: Choosing a linear model (e.g., Logistic Regression) to solve a complex, 
non-linear classification problem. 
o Over-Regularization: Applying excessively strong regularization (e.g., a very high 
$\lambda$ in Lasso), forcing the model coefficients toward zero and simplifying the function 
too much. 
 Impact on Generalization: High statistical bias leads to underfitting. The model performs poorly 
on both training and unseen test data because it fails to capture the underlying signal, resulting in a 
high, irreducible systematic error. 
2. Societal/Ethical Bias (Data Representation) 
This bias reflects unwanted systemic prejudices or skewed demographics present in the real world, which 
are then encoded into the model via the data. 
 Sources of Ethical Bias: 
o Selection Bias (Sampling Bias): When the training data is not representative of the real
world population the model will be deployed on. For instance, training a facial recognition 
model predominantly on light-skinned faces will cause it to perform poorly on darker-skinned 
faces. 
o Measurement Bias (Proxy Bias): When a sensitive feature (like race) is excluded, but the 
model learns to use a strongly correlated, non-sensitive feature (like ZIP code or purchasing 
habits) as an unethical proxy. 
o Historical Bias: Bias embedded in society's history, where outcomes reflect past 
discrimination (e.g., training a loan approval model on historical data where loans were 
unfairly denied to certain groups). 
 Impact on Generalization: Ethical bias leads to unfair generalization. The model performs 
accurately for the majority group present in the training data but systematically discriminates against 
or fails for minority or disadvantaged groups, leading to inequitable real-world consequences. 
A-b. Describe the role of assumptions in machine learning algorithms. Explain how 
assumptions influence model selection and performance. 
The role of assumptions in machine learning is central to the learning process; they are the necessary 
structural and statistical constraints that algorithms impose on the problem space to make the complex task 
of finding the true target function $f$ computationally tractable. This is encapsulated by the No Free Lunch 
theorem, which states that no single algorithm is universally superior across all possible problems—success 
depends on the degree to which the algorithm's assumptions align with the underlying structure of the data. 
How Assumptions Influence Model Selection 
Assumptions act as a guiding principle, influencing the choice between different models based on the nature 
of the data: 
1. Linearity: 
o Assumption: The relationship between features and the target variable can be approximated 
by a straight line or a hyperplane. 
o Models: Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, Linear SVM. 
o Selection Influence: Selected when the feature space is believed to be approximately 
separable by a line, prioritizing speed and interpretability. 
2. Independence (Parametric Assumptions): 
o Assumption: Features are independent of each other given the class. 
o Models: Naive Bayes. 
o Selection Influence: Chosen for high-dimensional, text-based data where speed is critical, 
despite the assumption of independence often being violated in reality. 
3. Local Smoothness (Non-Parametric Assumptions): 
o Assumption: Data points that are close to each other in the feature space are likely to have 
the same output value. 
o Models: K-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), Decision Trees. 
o Selection Influence: Chosen when the relationship is complex, non-linear, and the decision 
boundary is expected to be localized. 
How Assumptions Influence Performance 
The alignment between the model's assumptions and the true data generating process dictates performance: 
 Correct Alignment: When assumptions align with the data (e.g., using a Linear Regression model 
on truly linear data), the model benefits from high statistical efficiency (low bias) and performs 
optimally, generalizing well. 
 Misalignment (High Bias): If assumptions are too simplistic (e.g., assuming linearity for 
exponential data), the model suffers from high statistical bias and underfitting, systematically 
failing to model the data accurately. 
 Relaxed Assumptions (High Variance): Non-parametric models (like k-NN or unpruned Decision 
Trees) have fewer explicit assumptions, giving them high flexibility. While this reduces bias, it 
increases variance (sensitivity to training data noise), often leading to overfitting. 
A-c. Explain the concept of generalization in machine learning. Discuss why a model that 
performs well on training data may fail on unseen data. 
Generalization is the measure of a machine learning model's ability to accurately predict or classify 
outcomes for new, previously unseen data, having only been exposed to a finite training set. A model is said 
to generalize well if its performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, F1-score) on the independent test set closely 
mirror its performance on the training set. 
The Goal: Generalization Error $\approx$ Training Error 
The ultimate objective in machine learning is to minimize the Generalization Error (the expected error on 
all possible data points, often approximated by the Test Error). Achieving good generalization implies that 
the model has learned the underlying true signal or functional mapping in the data, rather than simply 
memorizing the noise and peculiarities of the training samples. 
Reasons for Failure on Unseen Data (Poor Generalization) 
A high training accuracy coupled with a low test accuracy signifies a large Generalization Gap and is the 
classic symptom of overfitting. This occurs primarily due to the following reasons: 
1. Overfitting to Noise (High Variance): 
o The model (e.g., a deep Neural Network or unpruned Decision Tree) is too complex relative 
to the size and quality of the training data. 
o The model learns the random errors, outliers, and noise specific to the training set. This 
memorization capacity, which is rewarded during training, makes the model brittle and 
incapable of handling the slight statistical variations present in the unseen test set. 
2. Insufficient or Non-Representative Data: 
o Data Scarcity: If the training set is too small, it may not adequately cover the feature space, 
leading the model to make wild extrapolations for unseen regions. 
o Sampling Bias: If the training data is not a statistically representative sample of the overall 
population (target domain), the learned function will be biased toward the training 
distribution and fail to generalize to the actual operational environment. 
3. Concept Drift: 
o Although often considered a deployment issue, it highlights a failure of generalization over 
time. The fundamental relationship between the input features ($X$) and the target variable 
($Y$) changes over time (e.g., customer behavior shifts), rendering the old model's learned 
rules invalid for the new, unseen data stream. 
A-d. Discuss the importance of data quality in machine learning systems. Explain how noise, 
missing values, and inconsistencies affect learning outcomes. 
Data quality is the most critical determinant of a machine learning system's success. As the famous adage 
goes, "Garbage In, Garbage Out" (GIGO). Low-quality data directly impacts the model's ability to learn 
the true underlying patterns, leading to biased, unstable, and ultimately non-deployable systems. 
1. Noise 
Noise refers to random errors or unwanted random variance in the measured values of features or the target 
variable. 
 Effect on Learning Outcomes: Noise increases the model's variance. Complex models, particularly 
those prone to overfitting (like deep neural networks or k-NN), will attempt to model this noise as a 
signal. This results in the model having poor generalization, as it learns random deviations specific to 
the training set, causing unstable predictions on unseen data. 
2. Missing Values 
Missing values occur when data is not recorded for one or more features in a sample. 
 Effect on Learning Outcomes: 
o Bias: Simply deleting records with missing data (list-wise deletion) can introduce significant 
selection bias if the data is not missing completely at random (MCAR). The model is then 
trained on a skewed, non-representative subset of the population. 
o Distortion: Using simple imputation methods (e.g., mean or median) distorts the true 
distribution of the feature and shrinks the true variance, leading to an artificially confident 
model. Advanced methods like predictive imputation are often required to mitigate this 
impact. 
3. Inconsistencies 
Inconsistencies involve contradictions in data formats, codes, or definitions across different records or 
datasets. 
 Effect on Learning Outcomes: 
o Model Confusion: Inconsistencies (e.g., 'NY' vs. 'New York' vs. 'N.Y.') are interpreted by 
the model as distinct categories, leading to unnecessary data sparsity and inefficient use of 
features. 
o Incorrect Relationships: Contradictory records (e.g., a patient recorded as male but having a 
historical record of pregnancy) corrupt the true functional relationship, undermining the 
model's ability to learn reliable dependencies between variables. 
A-f. Describe the ethical considerations involved in developing machine learning systems. 
Explain issues such as fairness, transparency, accountability, and privacy. 
The development and deployment of machine learning systems introduce complex ethical challenges that 
necessitate governance, technical diligence, and careful policy setting. These systems must be engineered 
not only for performance but also for societal benefit. 
1. Fairness and Bias 
 Explanation: Fairness refers to the principle that a machine learning model should not exhibit 
prejudice against individuals or groups based on sensitive characteristics (e.g., race, gender, 
religion). This is primarily compromised by Selection Bias or Historical Bias in the training data. 
 Ethical Issue: An unfair model can perpetuate and amplify real-world inequalities (e.g., an 
automated hiring tool systematically penalizing female candidates due to historical data skew). The 
definition of fairness itself is debated (e.g., equal opportunity vs. equal accuracy). 
2. Transparency and Explainability 
 Explanation: Transparency (or Explainability) is the ability to understand and articulate the internal 
logic and rationale behind a model's prediction. This is particularly difficult with high-capacity 
"black box" models like Deep Neural Networks. 
 Ethical Issue: Lack of transparency prevents stakeholders from determining why a decision was 
made. If a loan is denied or a medical diagnosis is given, the inability to explain the reason hinders 
redress, trust, and debugging for bias. Techniques like LIME and SHAP are used to address this. 
3. Accountability 
 Explanation: Accountability defines which party is responsible when a machine learning system 
causes harm or makes a costly error. This involves identifying the responsible agent—the data 
collector, the algorithm developer, the deploying entity, or the user. 
 Ethical Issue: In complex systems (e.g., autonomous vehicles), distributing liability is complicated. 
Clear lines of responsibility must be established to ensure that recourse and compensation are 
possible when algorithmic failures occur. 
4. Privacy 
 Explanation: Privacy relates to the protection of sensitive personal identifiable information (PII) 
used to train models. The risk is that the model itself might unintentionally memorize and reveal 
details about specific individuals in the training data. 
 Ethical Issue: Training models on sensitive data (e.g., medical records, financial history) risks data 
leakage. Privacy-enhancing technologies, such as Federated Learning (training models locally 
without sharing raw data) and Differential Privacy (adding controlled noise during training), are 
necessary to mitigate this risk.
