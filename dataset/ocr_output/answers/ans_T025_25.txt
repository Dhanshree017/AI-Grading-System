A-2. Describe the working of a Decision Tree algorithm. Explain how decisions are made 
and discuss its advantages and limitations. 
A Decision Tree is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm used for 
both classification and regression tasks. It uses a flowchart-like structure to model decisions 
and their potential consequences, mirroring human decision-making processes by breaking 
down complex choices into a series of simpler, sequential "if-then" questions.  
How the Algorithm Works 
The algorithm builds a tree through a process called recursive partitioning, where the 
dataset is repeatedly split into smaller subsets based on feature values.  
1. Root Node Selection: The process begins with the entire dataset at the "root" (topmost 
node). The algorithm evaluates all available features to find the one that best splits the data 
into the most distinct or "pure" groups. 
2. Splitting: The chosen feature divides the data into branches based on its possible values 
(e.g., "Income > $50,000" vs. "Income ≤ $50,000"). 
3. Recursive Branching: This splitting process is repeated for each resulting child node. At 
each step, the algorithm seeks the most significant attribute to further refine the subsets. 
4. Stopping Criteria: The splitting continues until a stopping condition is met, such as: 
1. All samples in a node belong to the same class (a "pure" node). 
2. A maximum tree depth is reached. 
3. The number of samples in a node falls below a certain threshold. 
5. Leaf Nodes: These are the final terminal nodes that do not split further; they represent the 
final decision or prediction.  
How Decisions (Splits) are Made 
Decisions are guided by Attribute Selection Measures (ASM), which identify the most 
informative features to maximize the homogeneity of the resulting subsets.  
 Entropy and Information Gain: Used primarily by the ID3 and C4.5 algorithms. Entropy 
measures the disorder or uncertainty in a dataset; Information Gain measures the reduction in 
entropy after a split. Features with the highest Information Gain are preferred. 
 Gini Impurity: Used by the CART algorithm. It measures the probability of a randomly 
chosen element being incorrectly classified. Splits that result in the lowest Gini Impurity 
(greatest purity) are chosen. 
 Variance Reduction: Used for regression tasks to minimize the variance of target values 
within each subset.  
Advantages 
 Interpretability: Decisions are easy to visualize and explain, even to non-experts, as they 
follow a clear logical path. 
 Minimal Data Preparation: Unlike many other algorithms, decision trees do not require 
data normalization, scaling, or extensive cleaning. 
 Versatility: They handle both numerical and categorical data naturally and can be used for 
both classification and regression. 
 Inbuilt Feature Selection: The algorithm inherently identifies the most important features 
during the construction process.  
Limitations 
 Overfitting: Deep trees can become overly complex, memorizing noise in the training data 
rather than general patterns, which leads to poor performance on new data. 
 Instability: Small changes in the training data can result in a completely different tree 
structure. 
 Biased Splits: Algorithms like ID3 are biased toward features with many levels (many 
possible values). 
 Greedy Approach: Because they make locally optimal decisions at each node, they do not 
guarantee a globally optimal tree.

false alarms, like saying someone is guilty when they are not. 
3. F1-Score: This is just a mix of the two above, to keep things balanced.

A-6. Describe how machine learning models are deployed and maintained in real-world 
systems. Explain issues such as monitoring, retraining, and performance degradation. 
In real-world systems, machine learning (ML) model deployment and maintenance are 
managed through a discipline known as MLOps (Machine Learning Operations). This 
process bridges the gap between experimental data science and production software 
engineering, ensuring models remain accurate, scalable, and reliable after they leave the 
development lab.  
1. Deployment Strategies 
Modern deployment involves packaging a trained model into a production environment 
where it can receive live data and return predictions.  
 Containerization: Models and their dependencies (libraries, code) are bundled into 
containers (e.g., Docker) to ensure they run identically across different environments. 
 Serving Patterns: 
o Real-time Inference: Models are hosted behind APIs (e.g., FastAPI, Flask) to provide 
instant predictions for applications like fraud detection or recommendation engines. 
o Batch Prediction: Models process large volumes of data at once on a schedule (e.g., daily 
sales forecasts). 
o Edge Deployment: Models run directly on local hardware like IoT devices or smartphones 
for low latency and offline use. 
 Release Strategies: Techniques like Canary Deployments (sending a small portion of 
traffic to the new model) or Blue-Green Deployments (instantly switching traffic between 
old and new versions) minimize the risk of system failures.  
2. Monitoring and Performance Degradation 
Unlike traditional software, ML models start to degrade almost immediately after 
deployment because they are sensitive to real-world changes.  
 Types of Degradation (Drift): 
o Data Drift: The statistical properties of input data change over time (e.g., customers 
suddenly buy different products due to a new trend). 
o Concept Drift: The fundamental relationship between inputs and outputs changes (e.g., what 
was previously considered "normal" behavior becomes "fraudulent" due to a new scamming 
technique). 
 Monitoring Key Metrics: 
o Model Performance: Tracking accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score in real-time. 
o System Health: Monitoring latency (speed of prediction), throughput, and resource usage 
like CPU/Memory. 
o Data Quality: Identifying missing values, outliers, or format changes in live data streams.  
3. Retraining and Continuous Maintenance 
To combat degradation, organizations must establish a plan for model updates.  
 Retraining Strategies: 
o Schedule-Based: Retraining happens at fixed intervals, such as weekly or monthly. 
o Trigger-Based: Retraining is automatically initiated when performance drops below a 
certain threshold or significant drift is detected. 
o Manual (Retrain on Demand): Teams manually intervene and retrain when they spot 
specific issues or new business requirements. 
 Automated Pipelines: MLOps systems use CI/CD (Continuous Integration/Continuous 
Deployment) to automate the entire lifecycle—from validating new data and retraining the 
model to testing it in a staging environment and redeploying it.

B-2. A bank wants to detect fraudulent transactions in real time. Describe how machine 
learning can be applied for fraud detection, including data sources, model behavior, 
and challenges involved. 
Machine learning (ML) enables banks to shift from reactive to proactive fraud detection, 
identifying suspicious activity in milliseconds rather than days. By analyzing massive 
datasets, ML models recognize complex, non-linear patterns and subtle anomalies that 
traditional rule-based systems often miss.  
1. Data Sources and Features 
Effective real-time detection relies on aggregating diverse data streams to create a "360
degree view" of a customer's behavior.  
 Transaction Metadata: Real-time stream data including amount, currency, timestamp, 
merchant category, and payment method. 
 Customer Profiles: Historical spending habits, average transaction values, and frequency of 
activity. 
 Geospatial & Device Data: Geolocation tracking, IP addresses, and unique device 
"fingerprints" to detect anomalies like "impossible travel" (e.g., card used in two distant 
locations within an hour). 
 Behavioral Biometrics: Analysis of user interaction patterns, such as typing speed, swipe 
gestures, and mouse movements. 
 External Data: Public records, social media profiles, and industry-shared blacklists of 
known fraudulent accounts.  
2. Model Behavior 
ML models function as adaptive filters that process incoming transactions through a series of 
analytical layers.  
 Supervised Learning: These models are trained on large, labeled datasets (e.g., past "fraud" 
vs. "legitimate" cases). Common algorithms include Random Forest (highly accurate for 
classification), XGBoost (effective for imbalanced data), and Logistic Regression (valued 
for speed and interpretability). 
 Unsupervised Learning: Used to discover new, previously unseen fraud tactics by 
clustering data based on similarities and flagging outliers. Algorithms like Isolation 
Forest and K-Means Clustering identify deviations from established normal behavior. 
 Real-Time Risk Scoring: The model assigns a probability score to each transaction. High
risk transactions are instantly blocked or diverted for multi-factor authentication (MFA), 
while low-risk ones proceed seamlessly.  
3. Key Challenges 
Implementing ML for real-time fraud detection involves significant technical and operational 
hurdles.  
 Massive Class Imbalance: Fraudulent transactions typically represent less than 0.2% of 
total activity. This imbalance can cause models to ignore rare fraud cases in favor of "safe" 
predictions unless specialized techniques like oversampling (e.g., SMOTE) are used. 
 Low Latency Requirements: Models must complete inference in milliseconds (often 
<100ms) to avoid disrupting the user experience at checkout. 
 Model Explainability ("Black Box"): Highly complex models, such as neural networks, 
can be difficult to interpret. Regulators often require banks to explain why a specific 
transaction was flagged. 
 Adaptive Adversaries: Fraudsters continuously evolve their tactics (e.g., using GenAI to 
mimic human behavior), requiring banks to frequently retrain and update models to prevent 
"model drift". 
 Data Privacy & Compliance: Banks must navigate strict regulations like GDPR and CCPA 
when processing sensitive financial data, often utilizing anonymization or federated learning 
to remain compliant.
