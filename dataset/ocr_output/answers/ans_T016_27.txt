A-1. What is data augmentation? Explain its importance in deep 
learning. 
Data augmentation is a technique used in machine learning, especially deep learning, to artificially increase 
the size, variety, and diversity of training datasets by applying modifications to existing data samples. 
Instead of collecting new data—which can be expensive and time-consuming—augmentation creates 
multiple transformed versions of the same data. These transformations are designed to preserve the original 
label while providing new variations for the model to learn from. 
In computer vision, augmentation includes transformations such as rotation, cropping, flipping, scaling, 
brightness changes, noise insertion, color jittering, affine transformations, and perspective warping. In NLP, 
augmentation includes synonym replacement, back-translation, random deletion, and word swapping. For 
audio datasets, augmentation may include pitch shifting, time stretching, noise addition, or equalization. 
The importance of data augmentation lies in its ability to improve generalization. Deep learning models, 
especially large neural networks, often overfit when the dataset is small or lacks diversity. Overfitting 
happens when the model memorizes training samples instead of learning generalizable patterns. 
Augmentation forces the model to learn robust representations by exposing it to varied input conditions. For 
example, a model trained on rotated or scaled images becomes invariant to orientation and size changes in 
real-world scenarios. Augmentation also introduces regularization since the model can no longer rely on 
exact pixel patterns. 
In many real applications—like medical imaging, satellite image analysis, and autonomous driving—
collecting millions of labeled examples is not feasible. Augmentation becomes essential in ensuring that the 
model performs well in unseen situations. Modern training pipelines for CNNs almost always include 
augmentation as a default step. With techniques like Mixup and CutMix, augmentation can even blend 
multiple images, allowing the model to learn smoother decision boundaries. 
Thus, data augmentation enhances dataset richness, reduces overfitting, improves generalization, and 
increases robustness, making it a crucial component of deep learning workflows. 
A-2. Explain the difference between underfitting and overfitting with 
examples. 
Underfitting and overfitting describe two opposite issues that arise during model training when the learned 
pattern fails to generalize to new data. They are central concepts in understanding model capacity, training 
quality, and generalization behavior. 
Underfitting occurs when a model is too simple, too restricted, or insufficiently trained to capture the 
underlying structure of the data. It learns only coarse patterns and fails to perform well on both training and 
testing sets. For example, fitting a straight line to a dataset that clearly follows a nonlinear curve leads to 
underfitting, because the model complexity is too low. A shallow model, limited features, or insufficient 
training iterations can cause this. An underfit decision tree that stops splitting too early or a neural network 
with too few layers also shows this behavior. Underfitting typically produces both high bias and high error. 
A-3. What is a decision boundary? Give an intuitive explanation with 
an example. 
A decision boundary is a surface—such as a line, curve, or hyperplane—that separates data points of 
different classes in the feature space. It represents the region where a model decides how to classify new 
inputs. Every classification algorithm implicitly or explicitly learns such a boundary based on training data. 
Intuitively, think of a 2D plane where each point represents a sample with two features. If we have two 
classes, say red and blue points, the classifier must “draw a boundary” that splits the red points from the blue 
ones. For simple linear models like logistic regression or linear SVM, the decision boundary is typically a 
straight line (or a hyperplane in higher dimensions). For nonlinear problems, algorithms such as kernel 
SVMs or neural networks produce curved or highly irregular boundaries that adapt to the shape of data. 
For example, suppose we are classifying emails as spam or not spam using only two features: frequency of 
suspicious words and email length. Plotting these values for all emails may show two clusters. A classifier 
might learn a boundary such as a slanted line that divides the clusters. Any new email falling on one side of 
the line is predicted as spam; the other side is non-spam. 
In neural networks, especially deep ones, decision boundaries can be extremely complex and high
dimensional. They consist of multiple interconnected linear segments formed by layers of neurons. This 
flexibility allows deep models to solve complicated classification tasks like object recognition. 
Thus, a decision boundary is the geometric separation learned by a classifier that determines the predicted 
class for any new input, making it a fundamental concept for understanding how classification models 
behave. 
A-4. Explain dropout and how it helps in regularizing neural 
networks. 
Dropout is a widely used regularization technique in neural networks that helps prevent overfitting by 
randomly “dropping” or disabling a subset of neurons during training. The idea is that at each training 
iteration, certain neurons are turned off with a fixed probability, meaning they do not contribute to forward 
or backward propagation. The network therefore cannot rely on specific neurons or connections and must 
develop more distributed and robust representations. 
During training, if dropout rate is 0.5, for example, roughly half of the neurons in a layer are temporarily 
removed. This forces the network to learn redundant paths so that information is not dependent on a small 
subset of parameters. At inference time (testing), dropout is not applied; instead, the weights are scaled so 
that the output remains consistent with the training distribution. 
Dropout acts like an ensemble method. Since different subsets of neurons are active in different iterations, 
training resembles training many smaller networks that share weights. The final model is effectively an 
average of these subnetworks, which leads to improved generalization. 
Without dropout, deep networks can easily memorize training data, especially when datasets are small or 
models are very deep. This results in low training loss but high testing error. Dropout reduces co
adaptation—where neurons depend excessively on each other—and encourages independence and 
robustness in learned representations. 
Another benefit is that dropout introduces noise into the network, which helps smooth the decision boundary 
and reduces sensitivity to small input changes. Variants like SpatialDropout (for CNNs) and DropConnect 
(drop weights instead of neurons) extend this idea further. 
Overall, dropout is a simple yet powerful regularization technique that improves neural network 
performance, especially when training data is limited or when models have large capacity.

B-1. Describe the Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm with its 
steps and applications. 
The Expectation–Maximization (EM) algorithm is an iterative optimization technique used for estimating 
parameters in statistical models where the data contains hidden, missing, or latent variables. Many 
probabilistic models cannot be solved analytically because part of the data is unobserved, making the 
likelihood function difficult or impossible to maximize directly. EM addresses this by alternating between 
estimating missing information and optimizing parameters. 
At its core, EM maximizes the likelihood of the observed data by leveraging the complete-data likelihood—
which includes both observed and hidden variables. The algorithm proceeds in two main steps, repeated 
until convergence 
Convergence 
The E and M steps are repeated until the parameters stop changing significantly. Although the EM algorithm 
is guaranteed to increase (or maintain) the likelihood at each step, it may converge to local maxima instead 
of a global maximum. 
B-2. Explain Dimensionality Reduction and compare PCA with 
Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA). 
Dimensionality reduction refers to the process of transforming data from a high-dimensional space into a 
lower-dimensional one while preserving as much relevant information as possible. High-dimensional data 
often contains redundancy, noise, and correlated features, which can lead to issues such as overfitting, 
increased computational cost, and poor visualization. Dimensionality reduction helps simplify the model, 
improve generalization, and speed up learning. 
Two major techniques for dimensionality reduction are Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear 
Discriminant Analysis (LDA). 
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) 
PCA is an unsupervised linear dimensionality reduction method. It identifies directions, called principal 
components, along which the data has maximum variance. These components represent the most 
informative axes of the dataset. 
Key ideas of PCA: 
 Computes the covariance matrix of data. 
 Finds eigenvalues and eigenvectors to determine directions of maximal variance. 
 Projects data onto top components with highest eigenvalues. 
PCA focuses solely on preserving variance and capturing structure, without considering class labels. 
B-3. Discuss Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). Explain the roles of the generator 
and discriminator. 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) are deep learning models designed to generate new data samples 
that resemble a given training dataset. Introduced by Ian Goodfellow in 2014, GANs use a unique two
network adversarial structure inspired by game theory. 
A GAN consists of two neural networks trained simultaneously: 
(A) Generator (G) 
The generator creates synthetic data samples. It takes random noise (e.g., Gaussian noise vector) as input 
and transforms it into a data sample resembling the real dataset. Its goal is to fool the discriminator into 
believing its output is real. 
(B) Discriminator (D) 
The discriminator is a binary classifier that distinguishes real data from fake (generated) data. It outputs a 
probability indicating whether the input is genuine. 
B-4. Explain the concept of Markov Decision Processes (MDP) in 
reinforcement learning and describe each component. 
A Markov Decision Process (MDP) provides a mathematical framework for modeling sequential decision
making problems where outcomes are partly random and partly controlled by an agent. MDPs form the 
foundation of reinforcement learning algorithms, as they describe how agents interact with environments 
through states, actions, and rewards. 
An MDP satisfies the Markov Property, meaning the future depends only on the current state, not on the 
full history. 
Formally, an MDP is defined by the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ): 
1. States (S) 
The set of all possible situations the agent can be in. 
Example: In a grid world, each cell is a state. 
2. Actions (A) 
The set of all possible moves the agent can take. 
Example: up, down, left, right. 
3. Transition Probability Function (P) 
Defines the probability of reaching a new state s′s's′ when taking action aaa in state sss: 
P(s′∣s,a)P(s'|s, a)P(s′∣s,a)  
This models the environment’s dynamics. 
4. Reward Function (R) 
Provides the immediate reward for transitioning from one state to another via an action. 
It defines the desirability of outcomes. 
5. Discount Factor (γ) 
A value between 0 and 1 that determines how much future rewards matter. 
Higher γ emphasizes long-term rewards. 
Why MDPs Are Important 
 They provide a structured way to define RL problems. 
 Algorithms like Value Iteration, Policy Iteration, Q-Learning, and Deep Q-Networks all operate on 
MDP formulations. 
 MDPs help compute optimal policies that maximize expected cumulative reward.

C-1. Define supervised learning with an example. 
Supervised learning is a machine learning approach where a model learns from labeled data. In labeled data, 
each input is paired with a correct output, allowing the algorithm to understand the relationship between 
features and the target variable. The main idea is to learn a mapping function f(x)→yf(x) \rightarrow 
yf(x)→y so that the model can predict outputs for new unseen inputs. 
Supervised learning can be divided into: 
 Classification: when the output variable is categorical. 
 Regression: when the output variable is continuous. 
How supervised learning works: 
1. A dataset containing input features and corresponding labels is provided. 
2. The model learns patterns from this data using algorithms such as Decision Trees, Logistic 
Regression, SVM, Neural Networks, etc. 
3. It then adjusts internal parameters to minimize errors. 
4. After training, the model is tested on unseen data to check its accuracy. 
Example: 
Spam email detection is a classic example. 
 Inputs: email text features 
 Output: “spam” or “not spam” label 
The model learns from thousands of labeled emails and becomes capable of predicting whether a new email 
is spam. 
Supervised learning is widely used in finance, healthcare, speech recognition, and recommendation systems. 
C-2. What is precision in classification metrics? 
Precision is a performance metric used in binary and multi-class classification that measures the accuracy of 
positive predictions. It answers the question: “Out of all the samples the model predicted as positive, how 
many were actually positive?” 
Precision is important especially in scenarios where false positives are costly. 
Mathematical definition: 
Precision=TPTP+FP\text{Precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}Precision=TP+FPTP  
Where: 
 TP (True Positives): correct positive predictions 
 FP (False Positives): incorrect positive predictions 
Why precision matters: 
 In medical diagnosis (e.g., cancer detection), a false positive may cause unnecessary stress and 
treatment. 
 In spam filtering, predicting a normal mail as spam is undesirable. 
Precision provides better insight in imbalanced datasets where one class dominates. 
Example: 
If a model predicts 50 emails as spam and 40 are actually spam, precision = 40 / 50 = 0.8 (80%). 
High precision means fewer false alarms.

C-4. Define feature scaling. 
Feature scaling is a preprocessing technique used to normalize or standardize the range of input features so 
that all variables contribute equally to model training. It is especially important in algorithms that rely on 
distance, gradient descent, or magnitude-based calculations. 
Why feature scaling is needed: 
 Features with large numerical ranges dominate those with small ranges. 
 Gradient descent converges faster when features share similar scales. 
 Distance-based models such as KNN or K-means produce more accurate results. 
 Algorithms like SVMs and Neural Networks become more stable. 
Two main types of feature scaling: 
1. Normalization (Min–Max Scaling): 
Rescales data to a range of [0,1][0, 1][0,1]. 
x′=x−xminxmax−xminx' = \frac{x - x_\text{min}}{x_\text{max} - x_\text{min}}x′=xmax−xminx−xmin  
Used in neural networks and KNN. 
2. Standardization (Z-score Scaling): 
Transforms data to mean 0 and standard deviation 1. 
x′=x−μσx' = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}x′=σx−μ  
Used in SVM, logistic regression, and PCA. 
Example: 
If “age” ranges from 10 to 90 and “income” ranges from 10,000 to 1,000,000, scaling ensures both influence 
model training equally. 
C-5. What is bagging? 
Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) is an ensemble machine learning technique that aims to improve model 
stability, reduce variance, and avoid overfitting. It works by training multiple models on different 
bootstrapped samples of the dataset and combining their outputs. 
How bagging works: 
1. Generate many bootstrapped samples by randomly selecting data points with replacement. 
2. Train separate models (usually Decision Trees) on each sample. 
3. Aggregate predictions: 
o Classification: majority voting 
o Regression: averaging 
Why bagging works: 
 Reduces variance because each model sees a different subset of data. 
 Helps unstable learners (like Decision Trees) generalize better. 
 Reduces chance of overfitting. 
Example: 
Random Forest is a classic bagging-based algorithm where each tree sees a different random dataset and 
random subset of features. 
Bagging is particularly powerful when working with noisy or high-variance models.
