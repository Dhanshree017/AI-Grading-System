A-1. Explain the role of validation sets in machine learning. Why is validation important, 
and how does it prevent model overfitting? 
Answer 
Definition / role: A validation set is a portion of labeled data reserved during development 
to evaluate model generalization while tuning hyperparameters and making decisions about 
model architecture or early-stopping. It is distinct from the training set (used to fit model 
parameters) and the test set (used for final evaluation). 
Why validation is important 
1. Hyperparameter selection: Validation performance guides selection of learning rate, 
regularization strength, depth/width, and other settings without peeking at the test set. 
2. Model comparison: It provides an unbiased comparison between candidate 
models/architectures before final evaluation. 
3. Early stopping: Monitoring validation loss/metric prevents training beyond the point 
where generalization degrades. 
4. Detects overfitting: A divergence between improving training metric and worsening 
validation metric signals that model is fitting noise. 
5. Robust model choices: Using cross-validation or repeated splits gives more reliable 
estimates and reduces variance in hyperparameter choices. 
A-2. Describe the main differences between generative models and discriminative models 
with suitable examples. 
Answer 
Definitions 
 Generative models: Model the joint distribution P(X,Y)P(X,Y)P(X,Y) or 
P(X)P(X)P(X) and can generate new samples. They capture how data is generated. 
 Discriminative models: Model the conditional distribution P(Y∣X)P(Y|X)P(Y∣X) or 
directly the decision boundary f(X)f(X)f(X) for prediction; they optimize 
classification/regression performance. 
Key differences 
1. Objective: Generative → model data generation; Discriminative → directly predict 
labels. 
2. Probability learned: Generative → P(X,Y)P(X,Y)P(X,Y) or P(X)P(X)P(X); 
Discriminative → P(Y∣X)P(Y|X)P(Y∣X). 
3. Sample generation: Generative can synthesize realistic samples (images, text); 
discriminative cannot. 
4. Complexity & data requirements: Generative often require more modeling capacity 
and data for realistic generation; discriminative models can be simpler for predictive 
tasks. 
5. Typical use-cases: Generative for data augmentation, simulation, unsupervised 
pretraining; discriminative for classification and regression with high accuracy. 
Examples 
 Generative: Naive Bayes (models class-conditional densities), Gaussian Mixture 
Models, Variational Autoencoders (VAE), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs). 
 Discriminative: Logistic Regression, Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random 
Forests, Feedforward Neural Networks fine-tuned for classification. 
Practical note: In classification tasks with abundant labeled data, discriminative models 
often yield better accuracy; when you need to model the data distribution or generate 
samples, generative models are appropriate. 
A-3. What is anomaly detection? Discuss its importance and common applications in real
world systems. 
Answer 
Importance 
1. Early warning: Detects faults, intrusions, or failures before they escalate. 
2. Risk management: Identifies fraudulent, erroneous, or malicious events that could 
cause financial or reputational damage. 
3. Safety & reliability: In industrial/medical contexts, catching anomalies can prevent 
accidents or patient harm. 
4. Operational efficiency: Helps find sensor malfunctions, data pipeline errors, and 
unusual system states that require corrective action. 
Common applications 
 Finance: Credit card fraud detection, anti-money laundering (unusual transaction 
patterns). 
 Cybersecurity: Intrusion and anomaly detection in network traffic, user behavior 
analytics. 
 Industry / Manufacturing: Predictive maintenance — detect abnormal 
vibration/temperature before machine failure. 
 Healthcare: Detect abnormal lab results, sepsis onset, or anomalous patient vitals. 
 Cloud/IT Operations: Detect traffic spikes, unusual API calls, or log anomalies 
indicative of incidents. 
 IoT & Sensors: Identify faulty sensors or environmental anomalies (e.g., sudden 
pollution spikes). 
Typical approaches 
 Statistical: Z-score, Gaussian models, control charts. 
 Proximity/density: k-NN, Local Outlier Factor, Isolation Forest. 
 Reconstruction-based: Autoencoders, PCA residuals — anomalies have high 
reconstruction error. 
 Time-series methods: Change-point detection, forecasting residuals, sequence models 
(LSTM) for temporal anomalies. 
A-4. Explain the concept of model interpretability. Why do certain industries require 
highly interpretable ML models? 
Answer 
Definition: Model interpretability is the extent to which a human can understand the cause 
of a decision made by a model — either globally (how the model behaves overall) or locally 
(why a single prediction was made). 
Why interpretability matters 
1. Trust & adoption: Users and stakeholders trust models they can understand. 
2. Regulatory compliance: Finance, healthcare, insurance and other regulated sectors 
require explanations for automated decisions. 
3. Accountability & auditability: Interpretability enables tracing decisions, attributing 
responsibility, and performing audits. 
4. Bias detection & fairness: Explanations reveal when models rely on protected or 
proxy features, enabling mitigation. 
5. Debugging & improvement: Interpretability helps identify data issues, spurious 
correlations, or model failure modes. 
Industries requiring interpretability 
 Healthcare: Clinicians need understandable rationale (e.g., key features leading to 
diagnosis). 
 Finance: Lending decisions, credit scoring must be explainable to customers and 
regulators. 
 Insurance: Risk assessment and pricing decisions need justification. 
 Legal & Governance: Automated decisions affecting rights or liberty require 
transparent reasoning. 
 Aerospace/Automotive (safety-critical): Explanations help ensure safe behavior and 
root-cause analysis. 
Methods for interpretability 
 Model-intrinsic: Use inherently interpretable models (linear models, decision trees, 
rule lists). 
 Post-hoc explainers: LIME (local surrogate), SHAP (Shapley value explanations), 
Partial Dependence Plots, saliency maps (for vision). 
 Counterfactuals: Show minimal changes to input that would change output — useful 
for recourse.

B-1. Explain the role of Data Preprocessing in building ML models. Discuss key steps 
such as data cleaning, encoding, transformation, and handling outliers. 
Answer 
Definition / purpose: Data preprocessing converts raw, noisy, and heterogeneous inputs into 
a consistent, high-quality dataset that a model can learn from effectively. Preprocessing often 
has larger impact on final performance than choice of algorithm. 
Key steps 
1. Data cleaning: Remove duplicates, correct typos and inconsistent formats, handle 
missing values (imputation strategies: mean/median/mode, KNN, model-based, or 
explicit missing indicator). 
2. Encoding categorical variables: Transform categories to numeric — one-hot for 
low-cardinality, ordinal encoding where order matters, frequency/target encoding or 
learned embeddings for high-cardinality features (use out-of-fold schemes to avoid 
leakage). 
3. Feature scaling & transformation: Standardization (zero mean/unit variance) or 
Min-Max scaling for gradient-based models; log or Box–Cox transforms to reduce 
skew; power transforms to stabilize variance. 
4. Handling outliers: Detect with IQR, z-score, or robust methods; treat by capping 
(winsorization), transformation, winsorization, or removing if erroneous; consider 
domain knowledge. 
5. Feature engineering: Create domain-specific features (ratios, interactions, 
aggregations, lag features for time series). 
6. Dimensionality reduction & sparsity handling: PCA, feature selection, hashing for 
high-cardinality sparse features. 
7. Pipelineization & reproducibility: Use pipelines to ensure identical preprocessing in 
training and production and avoid target leakage. 
Why it matters 
 Reduces noise and bias; improves convergence and model stability; reduces risk of 
data leakage; can dramatically increase predictive power with well-engineered 
features. 
B-2. Describe the importance of Model Evaluation Metrics. Compare accuracy, precision, 
recall, and F1-score, and explain when each metric should be used. 
Answer 
Overview: Choosing appropriate evaluation metrics aligns model optimization with business 
goals and risk profiles. A single metric can be misleading; choose metrics mindful of class 
imbalance and costs of different error types. 
Metrics 
1. Accuracy: (TP+TN)/Total(TP+TN)/Total(TP+TN)/Total. Good for balanced datasets 
where false positives and false negatives have similar costs. Poor choice for 
imbalanced datasets (e.g., 99% negative → trivial high accuracy). 
2. Precision (Positive Predictive Value): TP/(TP+FP)TP/(TP+FP)TP/(TP+FP). High 
precision means when model predicts positive, it is usually correct. Use when false 
positives are costly (e.g., spam incorrectly flagged as important email, unnecessary 
medical procedure). 
3. Recall (Sensitivity, True Positive Rate): TP/(TP+FN)TP/(TP+FN)TP/(TP+FN). 
High recall means model finds most real positives. Use when false negatives are costly 
(e.g., disease screening, fraud detection where missing real cases is dangerous). 
4. F1-score: Harmonic mean of precision and recall. Use when you need a balance and 
classes are imbalanced; gives a single-number trade-off. 
5. Additional: ROC-AUC for ranking ability; PR-AUC when positives rare; calibration 
measures for probabilistic predictions; cost-sensitive metrics or custom business utility 
functions when errors have different costs. 
When to choose 
 Accuracy: only when class balance and equal error cost. 
 Precision prioritized: when action on positives is expensive or harmful. 
 Recall prioritized: when catching positives is critical for safety. 
 F1-score: when both precision and recall matter and dataset is imbalanced. 
B-4. Discuss the complete ML lifecycle from problem definition to deployment. Explain 
the purpose of each stage with appropriate examples. 
Answer 
Stages & purpose 
1. Problem definition: Clarify business objective and constraints. Example: reduce 
churn by predicting customers likely to leave in 30 days. 
2. Data acquisition: Collect relevant data sources (CRM, logs, transactions). Ensure 
data quality and legal right for use. 
3. Data preprocessing & exploration: Clean, impute, detect bias, and perform 
exploratory analysis to understand distributions and relationships. 
4. Feature engineering: Create informative features (e.g., recency, frequency, monetary 
metrics for churn). 
5. Model development: Select algorithms, train models, tune hyperparameters using 
cross-validation. 
6. Evaluation & validation: Use validation/test sets and real-world metrics (ROC-AUC, 
recall@k). Include fairness checks and calibrations. 
7. Deployment: Serve the model in production (batch or real-time), integrate with 
application stack, set up APIs, and ensure latency/throughput constraints met. 
8. Monitoring & maintenance: Track model performance, data drift, and business 
KPIs; log inputs/predictions for audits. 
9. Retraining & iteration: Periodically retrain using fresh labeled data, update features, 
and deploy new versions with rollback safety. 
Example flow: Fraud detection system — define fraud types → collect transaction logs → 
engineer device/browser features → train ensemble model → evaluate precision/recall → 
deploy as real-time scoring API with human-in-loop review for high-risk transactions → 
monitor false positive rates and retrain weekly. 
B-5. What is Unsupervised Learning? Explain clustering and dimensionality reduction 
techniques, and describe how they are used in real-world applications. 
Answer 
Definition: Unsupervised learning finds patterns and structure in unlabeled data, such as 
grouping similar items or finding compact representations. 
Clustering 
 K-means: Partitions data into k clusters by minimizing within-cluster variance; fast 
but assumes spherical clusters. 
 DBSCAN: Density-based clustering that detects arbitrary-shaped clusters and noise 
(good for spatial data). 
 Hierarchical clustering: Builds dendrogram to view cluster relationships; useful 
when cluster granularity matters. 
Dimensionality reduction 
 PCA: Linear projection that captures directions of maximum variance, useful for 
denoising and feature reduction. 
 t-SNE / UMAP: Nonlinear embeddings for visualization preserving local 
neighborhood structure. 
 Autoencoders: Neural network-based compression and denoising. 
Real-world applications 
 Customer segmentation: Clustering customers by behavior for targeted marketing. 
 Anomaly detection: Identify outliers as rare clusters or noise. 
 Document clustering: Group similar documents or news for topic discovery. 
 Visualization: High-dimensional gene-expression data visualized with t-SNE/UMAP 
for biological insights. 
 Compression: Reduce features for downstream supervised training or edge 
deployment. 
B-6. Explain the concept of Reinforcement Learning. Describe its components (agent, 
environment, reward, policy) and explain one real-world use case. 
Answer 
Definition: Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a learning paradigm where an agent interacts 
with an environment, takes actions, and learns to maximize cumulative reward through trial
and-error. 
Components 
1. Agent: Learner/decision maker. 
2. Environment: The system agent interacts with; provides states and reward signals. 
3. State: Representation of environment at a time. 
4. Action: Choices available to the agent. 
5. Reward: Scalar feedback signal guiding behavior. 
6. Policy: Mapping from states to actions (deterministic or stochastic). 
7. Value function: Expected cumulative future reward for a state or state-action pair. 
8. Model (optional): A model of environment dynamics used for planning.

C-1. Discuss the challenges of deploying ML models in real-world environments. Explain 
issues like data drift, model monitoring, scalability, hardware constraints, security 
risks, and continuous retraining. 
Answer 
Overview: Deployment moves models from research into operation; this phase introduces 
engineering, operational, and governance complexities that can erode initial performance if 
unaddressed. 
Key challenges & solutions 
1. Data drift & distributional shift 
o Problem: Input or label distributions change over time (covariate, concept, 
target drift), degrading model accuracy. 
o Mitigation: Continuous monitoring of input statistics, PSI/K-S tests, model 
performance metrics; set retrain triggers; use adaptive models or ensemble of 
short/long-term models. 
2. Model monitoring & observability 
o Problem: Silent failures, calibration shifts, or rising error rates go unnoticed 
without monitoring. 
o Mitigation: Instrument logging of inputs/predictions/confidences, dashboards 
for metrics (latency, throughput, accuracy), alerting, and canary/blue-green 
deployments. 
3. Scalability & latency 
o Problem: Production loads and peak traffic require autoscaling and low-latency 
predictions. 
o Mitigation: Use optimized serving (TensorFlow Serving, TorchServe), model 
batching, caching, quantization, and horizontally scalable microservices. 
4. Hardware & resource constraints 
o Problem: Edge or mobile deployments have limited CPU, memory, and power. 
o Mitigation: Model compression (pruning, distillation, quantization), lightweight 
architectures (MobileNet), offloading heavy tasks to cloud. 
5. Security & robustness 
o Problem: Adversarial examples, model inversion, data poisoning, and API 
abuse threaten integrity. 
o Mitigation: Adversarial training, input sanitization, rate-limiting, authentication, 
differential privacy, monitoring for anomalous queries. 
6. Continuous retraining & lifecycle management 
o Problem: Model staleness; repeated retraining can introduce regressions. 
o Mitigation: MLOps pipelines for versioning data/models (DVC, MLflow), 
reproducible experiments, automated tests, CI/CD for models, and staged 
rollouts with performance gates. 
7. Governance, ethics & compliance 
o Problem: Regulatory requirements (GDPR, HIPAA), explainability, fairness 
audits. 
o Mitigation: Logging for audit trails, interpretable models or explanations, 
fairness testing across subgroups, and documented risk assessments. 
8. Integration & business alignment 
o Problem: Models must fit workflows and deliver measurable business value. 
o Mitigation: Prototype with product teams, measure business KPIs, include 
humans-in-loop for critical decisions. 
Conclusion: Robust deployment requires an ecosystem of monitoring, automation, 
governance, and engineering best practices, not just a performant model. 
C-2. Explain in detail how machine learning is used in healthcare. Describe applications 
(diagnosis, prediction, medical imaging, drug discovery), challenges (data quality, 
privacy, bias), and the steps involved in developing an ML-based healthcare system. 
Answer 
Applications 
1. Diagnosis & Screening: ML models (e.g., CNNs on imaging, ensemble models on 
EHR data) detect disease (cancer, diabetic retinopathy) often matching or exceeding 
human-level performance in specific tasks. 
2. Prognosis & Prediction: Models predict patient risk for readmission, sepsis onset, 
deterioration, enabling early intervention. 
3. Medical Imaging: Segmentation, detection, and quantification of lesions in X-ray, 
MRI, CT using deep learning. 
4. Drug Discovery: ML accelerates virtual screening, property prediction, de novo 
molecule generation (GANs/VAEs) and helps prioritize candidates for experimental 
testing. 
5. Operational Efficiency: Scheduling, resource optimization, and supply forecasting to 
improve hospital throughput. 
Steps to develop ML-based healthcare system 
1. Clinical need articulation: Define clinical question, outcome definition, and success 
metrics in collaboration with clinicians. 
2. Data collection & governance: Secure access to clinical data, ensure proper consent, 
and set up data governance. 
3. Data preprocessing & labeling: Clean data, standardize codes, and create high
quality labeled datasets (e.g., radiologist-annotated images). 
4. Exploratory analysis & feature engineering: Understand distributions, confounders, 
and design features (temporal aggregates, imaging features). 
5. Model development: Train models with cross-validation, use domain-aware 
architectures (e.g., CNNs for images, temporal networks for EHR), incorporate 
uncertainty estimates. 
6. Rigorous evaluation: Use clinically relevant metrics (sensitivity, specificity, PPV), 
subgroup analysis, calibration checks, and external validation cohorts. 
7. Clinical validation: Prospective studies, randomized controlled trials, or pilot 
deployments with clinician feedback. 
8. Regulatory submission & ethical review: Document performance, failure modes, 
and mitigation strategies for regulatory approval if required. 
9. Deployment & integration: Seamless EHR integration, decision support UI, human
in-loop mechanisms. 
10. Monitoring & maintenance: Post-deployment surveillance for drift, adverse events, 
continuous retraining with new labeled data, and periodic re-validation. 
Best practices: multidisciplinary teams (clinicians + data scientists + ethicists), external 
validation, transparency about limitations, and conservative deployment in high-stakes 
settings.
